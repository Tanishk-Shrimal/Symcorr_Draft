\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Motivation}

Lattice QCD provides a non-perturbative framework for studying the properties
and interactions of hadrons directly from quantum chromodynamics. In hadron
spectroscopy, the quantities of interest are commonly extracted from Euclidean
correlation functions constructed using interpolating operators with the
quantum numbers of the states being studied.

Modern spectroscopy calculations often require a basis containing several
interpolating operators rather than a single operator. Different operators may
contain different spin structures, momenta, spatial constructions, or
combinations of hadrons. Correlation matrices constructed from such operator
bases provide the information required to resolve the finite-volume energy
spectrum and to distinguish states with similar quantum numbers.

As the number and complexity of the interpolating operators increase,
constructing the corresponding correlation functions becomes increasingly
cumbersome. For every combination of source and sink operators, the allowed
Wick contractions must be determined, fermionic signs must be treated
consistently, and the flavor, spin, color, and spatial structures must be
carried through to the numerical calculation. Although many of these steps are
systematic, performing them manually for every new operator basis requires
substantial implementation effort and can introduce bookkeeping errors.

The distillation framework~\cite{Peardon:2009gh} provides a particularly useful
setting for spectroscopy calculations because it separates quark propagation
from the operator-dependent structures used to construct hadrons. This makes
it possible to construct a wide range of interpolating operators from a common
set of quark-propagation data. Nevertheless, the transformation from the
mathematical definition of an interpolating operator to the complete numerical
expression for its correlation function remains a non-trivial task.

\textsc{SymCorr} is developed to automate this transformation. Interpolating
operators are first expressed symbolically, after which the package determines
the corresponding Wick contractions and systematically transforms the
resulting expressions toward their numerical representation in the
distillation framework. The aim is to reduce the amount of correlator-specific
algebra and implementation that must be performed manually, while keeping the
physical definition of an operator separate from the details of its numerical
evaluation.

\subsection{Challenges in lattice QCD correlator construction}

For a simple interpolating operator, constructing a two-point correlation
function can be relatively straightforward. A meson operator, for example,
contains one quark and one antiquark field, and the corresponding Wick
contractions involve only a small number of quark lines. The structure becomes
more involved when the operators contain several quark fields or when many
operators are combined into a correlation matrix.

For a basis of interpolating operators \(\{\mathcal O_i\}\), one evaluates the
matrix of correlation functions

\begin{equation}
C_{ij}(t)
=
\left\langle
\mathcal O_i(t)\,\mathcal O_j^{\dagger}(0)
\right\rangle .
\end{equation}

Each source--sink pair can contain different flavor, spin, momentum, and
spatial structures. These structures must be retained consistently while all
allowed contractions between quark and antiquark fields are identified. When
several fields of the same flavor are present, there can be multiple valid
pairings, and the relative signs associated with fermionic permutations must
also be handled correctly.

The amount of bookkeeping therefore grows as one moves from simple meson
operators to baryons and multi-hadron operators. At the same time, realistic
spectroscopy calculations require many source--sink combinations, so the same
basic contraction patterns and operator-dependent structures can appear
repeatedly across different matrix elements.

After the Wick contractions have been determined, the resulting propagator
expressions must still be translated into the objects used for numerical
evaluation. In the distillation framework this means expressing the correlator
in terms of perambulators and operator-dependent structures in the Laplacian
eigenvector space. Carrying out this translation separately for each operator
combination makes the implementation difficult to extend and increases the
amount of repeated algebra that must be checked by hand.

These difficulties motivate a systematic representation of the operators and
their contractions. In \textsc{SymCorr}, the operator definition, Wick
contraction, and subsequent translation to the distillation representation are
treated as distinct stages. This allows each stage to be constructed and
verified independently before introducing the numerical optimizations required
for large correlation matrices.

\subsection{Related software and prior approaches}

Several tools address different parts of the operator-construction and
correlator-evaluation workflow in lattice QCD. The Multi-Hadron Interpolators
(MHI) package implements a general procedure for combining operators with
definite momenta and projecting them into irreducible representations of the
appropriate lattice little groups~\cite{PhysRevD.109.094516}. More recently,
\texttt{OpTion} provides a systematic construction of multi-hadron lattice
operators with general momentum, spin, and internal quantum numbers, with
explicit constructions for systems containing up to four hadrons
~\cite{Yan:2025op}. These approaches address the important problem of building
interpolating operators with the required finite-volume symmetry properties.

A separate class of work focuses on the subsequent evaluation of correlation
functions. The Redstar analysis framework represents Wick-contraction terms as
graphs and uses graph-contraction algorithms to reduce redundant work and to
organize tensor contractions for execution on CPUs and GPUs
~\cite{10.1145/3592979.3593409}. Its formulation includes tensors carrying
both distillation and spin indices, and therefore provides an important example
of optimized correlator evaluation in a distillation-based calculation.
Related algorithmic work has also explored determinant representations of Wick
contractions and methods for identifying smaller sets of determinant terms that
are sufficient for evaluating nuclear correlation functions
~\cite{PhysRevD.110.114505}.

These developments address complementary parts of a broad computational
problem. The emphasis of \textsc{SymCorr} in the present work is on treating
the complete transformation from a symbolic quark-level operator description
to a numerical distillation calculation as a sequence of explicit compilation
stages. Operator representation, Wick contraction, translation to
backend-independent physics objects, distillation lowering, execution planning,
and numerical evaluation are kept as separate layers of the software. This
staged organization is intended to make the algebraic transformations easier to
inspect and validate while allowing numerical execution strategies to evolve
without changing the original physics description of the interpolating
operators.

\subsection{Scope and contributions of SymCorr}

\textsc{SymCorr} is intended to operate on the part of a distillation-based
calculation that begins once the required lattice data are available. In
particular, it contracts precomputed numerical objects such as Laplacian
eigenvectors and perambulators; it does not generate the Laplacian eigenvectors
or perform inversions of the Dirac operator. Its role is to connect the
high-level specification of interpolating operators to the construction and
numerical evaluation of the corresponding correlation functions.

The main contributions of the present implementation are:

\begin{itemize}

\item A symbolic representation of lattice-QCD interpolating operators together
with automatic Wick contraction, so that the quark-field content and allowed
fermionic contractions can be handled before numerical storage or execution
details are introduced.

\item A staged compilation pipeline in which the contracted expressions are
first translated into a backend-independent physics representation and are then
lowered to the quantities required by the distillation formalism. This keeps
the physics-level transformations separate from the later numerical execution
choices.

\item A global representation of the numerical contraction problem that makes
repeated structures visible across the calculation. The current implementation
uses this representation for contraction-order selection, reuse of common
intermediate quantities, scheduling, and memory planning before numerical
execution.

\item A numerical runtime that binds the compiled calculation to externally
supplied lattice data and evaluates the resulting contractions on CPU or GPU.
The execution layer includes automatic hardware and memory checks, while the
symbolic physics description remains independent of the machine on which the
calculation is run.

\item A validation strategy that separates algebraic and compiler regression
tests from comparison with independent lattice-QCD data. At present, the
external-data validation envelope includes rest- and moving-frame single-meson
correlators, several bilocal two-hadron correlators, and a tested local
four-quark contraction with a known external normalization difference. Other
implemented pathways are not assumed to have the same level of numerical
validation unless stated explicitly later in this work.

\end{itemize}

This distinction between implemented functionality and externally validated
physics calculations is maintained throughout the paper. Performance claims
are likewise deferred to controlled benchmarks presented separately, rather
than being inferred from the existence of individual optimization stages.

\section{Physics and Computational Background}
\label{sec:background}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

% This section contains only the background needed to understand the
% construction and evaluation problem addressed by SymCorr.

\subsection{Correlation functions and operator bases}

In Euclidean lattice QCD, hadronic information is obtained from correlation
functions built from interpolating operators. For a sink operator
\(\mathcal O_i\) and a source operator \(\mathcal O_j^\dagger\), the two-point
correlation function is

\begin{equation}
C_{ij}(t,t_0)
=
\left\langle
\mathcal O_i(t)\,\mathcal O_j^\dagger(t_0)
\right\rangle ,
\label{eq:two_point_matrix}
\end{equation}

where \(t_0\) and \(t\) denote the source and sink times, respectively. The
operators are constructed to carry the quantum numbers of the finite-volume
states of interest.

A single interpolating operator generally couples to more than one state with
the same quantum numbers. Schematically, and suppressing finite-temporal-extent
effects, the correlation matrix has the spectral form

\begin{equation}
C_{ij}(t,t_0)
=
\sum_n
Z_i^{(n)} Z_j^{(n)*}
 e^{-E_n(t-t_0)},
\qquad
Z_i^{(n)}=\langle 0|\mathcal O_i|n\rangle .
\label{eq:spectral_decomposition}
\end{equation}

Using several operators with different structures therefore provides different
overlaps with the same set of finite-volume states. This is the reason that
spectroscopy calculations commonly work with a correlation matrix rather than
a single correlator. The subsequent extraction of the spectrum is an analysis
problem; the role considered in this work is the construction and evaluation
of the matrix elements \(C_{ij}\) themselves.

If the sink basis contains \(N_{\mathrm{sink}}\) operators and the source basis
contains \(N_{\mathrm{source}}\) operators, then each source--sink time pair
requires up to
\(N_{\mathrm{sink}}N_{\mathrm{source}}\) correlator matrix elements. The two
bases may be the same, in which case the familiar square correlation matrix is
obtained, or they may be specified independently. In either case, every matrix
element must preserve the flavor, spin, momentum, spatial, and source--sink
orientation carried by its two operators.

This matrix structure is important computationally. Different matrix elements
are not unrelated calculations: they can share quark-propagation data,
operator-dependent building blocks, and portions of the resulting contraction
expressions. Treating the complete operator basis as a structured calculation,
rather than evaluating each \(C_{ij}\) as an isolated expression, therefore
creates opportunities to identify repeated work while maintaining a single
consistent definition of the underlying operators and their contractions.

\subsection{Distillation formalism}

The distillation method~\cite{Peardon:2009gh} introduces a gauge-covariant
spatial smearing operator constructed from a low-dimensional subspace of the
three-dimensional gauge-covariant Laplacian. On each time slice, let
\(v^{(k)}(t)\) denote the retained Laplacian eigenvectors and collect them as the
columns of the matrix

\begin{equation}
V(t)
=
\left(
 v^{(1)}(t),\ldots,v^{(N_v)}(t)
\right),
\end{equation}

where \(N_v\) is the number of retained modes. The distillation operator is the
projector onto this subspace,

\begin{equation}
\Box(t)=V(t)V^\dagger(t).
\label{eq:distillation_projector}
\end{equation}

Applying this projector to a quark field gives the distillation-smeared field

\begin{equation}
\widetilde q(t)=\Box(t)q(t),
\qquad
\widetilde{\bar q}(t)=\bar q(t)\Box(t),
\end{equation}

with the spin indices left untouched by the spatial projector.

The corresponding quark propagation can then be represented inside the
retained Laplacian subspace. For quark flavor \(f\), with Dirac operator
\(M_f\), the perambulator is

\begin{equation}
\tau_f(t,t_0)
=
V^\dagger(t) M_f^{-1}(t,t_0) V(t_0).
\label{eq:perambulator}
\end{equation}

The perambulator carries the Laplacian-mode indices together with the quark
spin indices. Importantly, once the flavor, gauge configuration, source time,
and sink time are fixed, it is independent of the detailed interpolating
operator in which it will later appear. The same propagation data can therefore
be reused when constructing many operators and many elements of a correlation
matrix.

The operator-dependent spatial and spin structure is represented separately.
For a generic single-hadron building block \(A(t)\), which may contain a Dirac
matrix, a momentum phase, and spatial displacement or derivative structure, its
projection into the distillation space has the form

\begin{equation}
\Phi_A(t)
=
V^\dagger(t)\,A(t)\,V(t).
\label{eq:elemental}
\end{equation}

Such projected operator-dependent objects are commonly referred to as
\emph{elementals}. Their detailed tensor structure depends on the interpolating
operator, whereas the perambulators contain the quark propagation between time
slices. Correlation functions in the distillation framework are consequently
assembled from contractions of these two classes of objects.

This separation is the feature of distillation most relevant to the present
work. The expensive quark-propagation information can be prepared independently
of a particular spectroscopy operator basis, while changes to momenta, spin
structures, or spatial constructions modify the operator-dependent factors.
\textsc{SymCorr} assumes the required Laplacian eigenvectors and propagation
data are supplied externally and automates the subsequent transformation from
the symbolic operator definition to the corresponding distillation-space
objects and contractions.

\subsection{From an interpolating operator to a numerical correlator}

The starting point of a correlator calculation is the mathematical definition
of the source and sink interpolating operators. Each operator is an algebraic
combination of quark and antiquark fields together with the structures required
to specify its flavor, spin, momentum, color, and spatial content. Forming the
product appearing in Eq.~\eqref{eq:two_point_matrix} therefore produces an
ordered product of fermion fields and operator-dependent factors.

The first transformation is the application of Wick's theorem. Quark and
antiquark fields of compatible flavor are paired, and each valid complete
pairing contributes a term with the fermionic sign determined by the required
permutation of fields. A contracted quark--antiquark pair is replaced by the
corresponding quark propagator,

\begin{equation}
q_f(x)\,\bar q_f(y)
\longrightarrow
M_f^{-1}(x,y).
\label{eq:wick_to_propagator}
\end{equation}

After all valid pairings have been generated, the correlation function is an
exact sum of terms containing quark propagators together with the spin, color,
momentum, and spatial structures inherited from the source and sink operators.
At this stage the expression is already determined by the physics definition
of the correlator, but it has not yet been organized for numerical evaluation.

Within the distillation framework, every propagator in this expression is then
projected into the retained Laplacian subspace and represented by a
perambulator, while the operator-dependent factors are projected into the
corresponding distillation-space objects. The result can be written
schematically as

\begin{equation}
C_{ij}(t,t_0)
=
\sum_{\alpha}
 c_{\alpha}\,
\operatorname{Contr}
\left[
\{\Phi\}_{\alpha},
\{\tau\}_{\alpha}
\right],
\label{eq:distillation_contraction_schematic}
\end{equation}

where \(\alpha\) labels the Wick-contraction terms, \(c_{\alpha}\) contains the
associated numerical coefficient and fermionic sign, and
\(\operatorname{Contr}\) denotes the required contractions over spin,
Laplacian-mode, and any remaining internal indices. The detailed set of tensors
and contractions depends on the interpolating operators, but the overall
transformation is common to all correlators.

A numerical calculation must finally realize the tensors required for the
chosen gauge configuration and source--sink times and evaluate the contractions
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:distillation_contraction_schematic}. At this point there can be
many mathematically equivalent ways to order the tensor operations, and several
correlator terms or matrix elements can require the same intermediate objects.
These choices affect computational cost and memory use, but they do not change
the underlying correlation function.

This separation between the physics transformation and its numerical execution
is central to \textsc{SymCorr}. The operator definition determines the Wick
contractions and the distillation-space expression, whereas the subsequent
choice of contraction ordering, reuse, memory placement, and execution device
can be treated independently. The remainder of this paper describes how these
stages are represented and connected in the software.

\section{Overview of SymCorr}
\label{sec:overview}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Design goals}

The design of \textsc{SymCorr} is guided by the requirement that the physical
definition of a correlation function remain separate from the details of its
numerical realization. A user should be able to specify the interpolating
operators and the correlator to be evaluated without also specifying how the
resulting tensor contractions will be ordered, where intermediate arrays will
be stored, or which processor will execute them. These numerical choices may
change between machines without changing the operator definition.

A first design goal is therefore to preserve a physics-level description for as
long as possible. Flavor, spin, color, momentum, spatial transformations,
operator ordering, and source--sink orientation are established before backend
or storage decisions are introduced. As the calculation is transformed into
more concrete representations, later stages are required to preserve these
established physical meanings rather than infer or reinterpret them from
numerical tensor layouts.

A second goal is to make the systematic algebra explicit and independently
verifiable. Wick contraction, the identification of quark-propagation
structure, and the translation to the distillation representation are treated
as separate transformations. This separation makes it possible to test each
stage against its own mathematical requirements and to reject unsupported or
ambiguous constructions at the stage where they arise, rather than allowing a
numerical backend to guess the intended physics.

The third goal is to treat a correlation matrix as a single structured
computational problem. As discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:background}, different
matrix elements can share propagation data, operator-dependent structures, and
intermediate contractions. SymCorr therefore retains the relationships among
the requested correlators long enough to identify repeated mathematical work
across the full calculation, instead of compiling every matrix element as an
independent numerical program.

A fourth goal is to separate mathematical optimization from deployment. Choices
such as contraction ordering and reuse of algebraically identical quantities
are properties of the tensor-contraction problem itself. By contrast, choices
such as whether a calculation fits in device memory, how much work can be
processed together, or whether execution should occur on a CPU or GPU depend on
the resources available when the calculation is run. Keeping these decisions in
separate stages allows the same physics calculation to be evaluated on
different hardware without embedding machine-specific assumptions into the
symbolic description.

Finally, the framework is designed so that increasingly sophisticated execution
strategies do not require changes to the operator language. Memory planning,
time-context batching, data reuse, and hardware-aware execution can therefore
be developed below the physics boundary. This provides a practical route for
improving numerical performance while retaining one inspectable and testable
definition of the correlation function throughout the compilation process.

At the first stage, the user supplies the source and sink operator definitions
and specifies the correlator or correlation matrix to be constructed. These
objects contain the physical information required to define the calculation,
but they do not contain contraction paths, memory layouts, or device-specific
execution choices.

The second stage applies the symbolic transformations required by the field
theory. The operator expressions are put into the form expected by the Wick
engine, all allowed quark--antiquark pairings are generated, and the associated
fermionic signs and coefficients are retained. The output of this stage is an
exact contracted expression, still independent of how the required propagators
will be stored or evaluated numerically.

The contracted expression is then translated into a backend-independent
physics representation and subsequently into the resources required for a
distillation calculation. Quark lines are associated with propagation
resources, while the operator-dependent spatial, color, and spin information is
converted into the corresponding projected structures. This stage establishes
which mathematical tensors and index connections are required without yet
assigning them to concrete files, devices, or memory buffers.

Once the numerical tensor problem is known, the requested correlators are
considered together as a global contraction problem. Repeated resources and
reusable computations can be identified across Wick terms and correlation-matrix
elements, contraction orders can be selected, and the resulting operations can
be scheduled with an associated memory plan. These transformations reorganize
an already determined mathematical calculation; they do not alter the
correlator defined by the earlier stages.

Concrete lattice data enter at run time. The compiled resource requirements are
bound to the supplied Laplacian eigenvectors, perambulators, and other supported
external data, after which the required primitive tensors are materialized.
Deployment decisions such as a feasible CPU or single-GPU execution mode and
memory-compatible batching are made using the resources available on the
machine where the calculation is run. The stored tensor operations are then
evaluated to produce the requested complex correlator values.

A practical consequence of this separation is that the compiled mathematical
calculation and the data used to evaluate it have different roles. The former
records what contractions must be carried out; the latter provide the
configuration- and time-dependent numerical values needed for a particular
execution. This distinction allows a compiled calculation to remain portable
across compatible runs while hardware feasibility and data placement are
resolved only when numerical execution is requested.

\subsection{Current scope}

The present implementation has a broader compiler and runtime capability than
its independently validated production envelope. These two notions of scope
are therefore distinguished explicitly in this work.

The strongest validation presently applies to exact-distillation two-point
calculations using the supported HDF5 data adapters. Complete complex
correlators have been compared with independent Chroma XML/HDF5 results for
rest- and moving-frame single mesons and for a set of rest- and moving-frame
bilocal two-hadron operators. For the tested nonzero source--sink separations,
the single-meson and bilocal results agree with the external calculation at
approximately machine precision. These pathways define the current
production-ready validation envelope.

A local four-quark \(T_{1g}/T_1^+\) contraction has also been tested at
\(N_v=45\). Its complex value agrees with the independent reference after the
known external operator-normalization relation is taken into account. SymCorr
retains its native mathematical normalization and does not insert an automatic
global volume correction. The corresponding \(N_v=60\) calculation was not
completed because of memory pressure. The four-quark result is therefore an
important numerical validation case, but it is not assigned the same
production status as the single-meson and bilocal pathways.

Beyond this external-data envelope, the compiler and runtime contain additional
capabilities. Representative meson, baryon, and tetraquark calculations are
used in compiler and runtime regression fixtures, and generalized/POINT-current
propagation resources together with disconnected or closed-loop paths are
represented in the current pipeline. These tests establish software behavior
and algebraic consistency, but they are not substitutes for comparison with
independent lattice data. Accordingly, these pathways are treated as
implemented capabilities whose physics validation remains separate.

The current runtime executes within a single process. Hardware discovery can
inspect the CPUs, host memory, and CUDA devices visible to the process, and the
deployment layer can choose a resident CPU execution, a resident single-GPU
execution, or a host-staged single-GPU execution when appropriate. It does not
currently shard one calculation over multiple GPUs or CPUs, spill beyond host
memory, or provide MPI/multi-process HDF5 execution. These are execution
boundaries rather than restrictions on the symbolic definition of the
correlator.

Finally, SymCorr begins after the expensive gauge-field inputs required by a
distillation calculation have been produced. The package consumes externally
supplied Laplacian eigenvectors, perambulators, and other supported numerical
resources; generation of the Laplacian eigenvectors and inversion of the Dirac
operator are outside its scope. Later sections retain these distinctions when
describing examples, validation, and performance so that implemented
functionality is not conflated with independently validated production use.

\section{Symbolic Representation of Interpolating Operators}
\label{sec:symbolic}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Elementary symbolic objects}

The symbolic frontend represents an interpolating operator as an ordered
algebraic expression assembled from objects that correspond directly to the
field-theory ingredients of the operator. At this level the representation is
independent of distillation tensors, external data layouts, contraction paths,
and execution backends. The purpose of the frontend is to retain enough
physical structure that subsequent compiler stages can determine these details
systematically.

The elementary fermionic objects are indexed quark and antiquark fields. A
field of flavor \(f\) is represented schematically as

\begin{equation}
q_f(x)_{a\alpha},
\qquad
\bar q_f(x)_{a\alpha},
\label{eq:symbolic_quark_field}
\end{equation}

where \(x\), \(a\), and \(\alpha\) denote position, color, and spin indices,
respectively. The flavor and the distinction between a quark and an antiquark
are properties of the field itself rather than annotations added during Wick
contraction. This allows flavor compatibility and fermion ordering to be
established directly from the symbolic expression.

Spin structure is represented by indexed matrices
\(\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}\). The frontend provides the usual gamma matrices and
also permits named spin matrices used by the supported operator constructions,
including charge-conjugation structures. At the symbolic level the important
information is the matrix identity together with its two spin indices; the
concrete numerical representation of the spin matrices is a later convention
and does not alter the operator expression.

Color contractions are explicit symbolic tensors. The current frontend contains
both the Kronecker tensor \(\delta_{ab}\) and the antisymmetric tensor
\(\epsilon_{abc}\), which provide the basic color structures required by the
implemented meson- and baryon-like constructions. Gauge transport can also be
represented explicitly through a gauge-link object
\(U(x,y)_{ab}\) carrying its endpoints and open color indices. Keeping these
objects visible in the expression prevents color connectivity from being
inferred later from the ordering or shape of numerical arrays.

Spatial transformations acting on individual fields are represented separately
from the fields themselves. The current frontend distinguishes a pure
coordinate shift from gauge-covariant parallel transport, provides a symbolic
covariant derivative along a directed lattice axis, and supports field-level
spatial projections such as the LapH and point representations. These
transformations are attached to a particular field expression rather than being
silently absorbed into the field. Consequently, an object such as
\(D_\mu q_f\) retains the fact that a derivative acts on that specific field as
the expression proceeds through the compiler.

Products of the elementary objects form larger symbolic expressions, with the
factor ordering retained explicitly. This is essential for fermionic
expressions because the ordering of quark fields enters the sign obtained after
Wick contraction. A complete interpolating operator then wraps such an
expression together with its momentum assignment or assignments and optional
metadata. For operators with several spatial locality groups, momentum can be
associated with the corresponding position labels rather than being forced into
one global momentum value.

A quark propagator is deliberately not part of the user-authored symbolic
vocabulary. Propagator objects arise only after the source and sink expressions
have been combined and Wick contraction has paired compatible quark and
antiquark fields. This boundary ensures that the user specifies the
interpolating operator itself, while propagation topology is derived from the
field content rather than supplied manually.

\subsection{Indices and quantum numbers}

SymCorr assigns every symbolic index to one of three spaces: spin, color, or
position. An index is therefore not identified by its text label alone. When a
label is first introduced, its index space is fixed; attempting to reuse the
same label as a different type is rejected. Reusing the label within the same
space refers to the same symbolic index and hence defines the intended tensor
connectivity of the operator.

Spin and color indices follow the usual Einstein-style interpretation within a
symbolic expression. An index appearing twice denotes a contracted index, while
a single occurrence remains open. Open spin indices are permitted because, for
example, baryon operators can carry an uncontracted Dirac index. An uncontracted
color index is allowed by the representation but is reported as a warning
because the resulting operator may not be gauge invariant. Spin or color
indices appearing more than twice are rejected rather than assigned an
ambiguous contraction meaning.

Position indices play a different role. They identify the spatial or spacetime
locality associated with fields and gauge structures and are therefore not
subject to the same multiplicity rule. Two fields carrying the same position
label belong to the same locality group, whereas distinct labels can be used to
represent bilocal or more general composite operators. In this sense the
position labels participate in the physical specification of the operator even
though their literal names are dummy labels.

The frontend does not combine all quantum numbers into a single opaque tag.
Flavor is stored directly on each quark field, spin and color information are
encoded by the indexed fields and tensors, and momentum is attached to the
operator through its position-locality groups. If an operator contains exactly
one such group, a single three-component lattice momentum is unambiguous. If it
contains several independent locality groups, the momentum assignment is given
as a map from the corresponding position labels to three-component momenta,

\begin{equation}
 x_r \longmapsto \mathbf p_r,
 \qquad
 \mathbf P = \sum_r \mathbf p_r ,
\label{eq:operator_momentum_groups}
\end{equation}

where \(\mathbf P\) is the total momentum carried by the composite operator.
This representation keeps the momentum associated with each constituent
locality visible rather than replacing it immediately by the total momentum.
Under Hermitian conjugation, every stored momentum assignment changes sign,
consistent with the reversed source--sink orientation.

The literal names chosen for dummy spin, color, and position indices are not
part of the mathematical identity of an operator. SymCorr compares operator
structure after a deterministic relabeling of these indices while preserving
the ordered expression and the associated momentum assignments. Thus two
operators that differ only by dummy-index names can be recognized as the same
symbolic structure, whereas a change of flavor, tensor connectivity, factor
ordering, spatial transformation, or momentum remains physically significant.

A second use of relabeling occurs when several operators are inserted into one
correlator. Each insertion is placed in a distinct index namespace before Wick
contraction. The corresponding position labels in the momentum map are renamed
at the same time. This prevents a source index and a sink index from becoming
accidentally identified merely because the two independently constructed
operators happened to use the same dummy label. Intentional contractions
inside each operator remain unchanged.

For a two-point correlation matrix, the sink and source bases are retained as
separate ordered objects. When an explicit source basis is not supplied, the
source operators are constructed as the Hermitian conjugates of the sink
operators; otherwise the two bases may be specified independently. This keeps
source--sink orientation, flavor content, momentum, and open-index structure
explicit before the Wick-contraction stage determines the allowed propagation
lines.

\subsection{Constructing composite operators}

Composite operators are constructed by multiplying the elementary symbolic
objects introduced above while preserving their order and typed-index
connectivity. This gives one common representation for operator structures of
different hadronic complexity rather than requiring a separate symbolic
language for each hadron family. Schematically, a local meson-like operator may
have the form

\begin{equation}
\mathcal O_M(x)
=
\bar q_{f_1}(x)_{a\alpha}
\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}
q_{f_2}(x)_{a\beta},
\label{eq:schematic_meson_operator}
\end{equation}

whereas a local three-quark structure can be written as

\begin{equation}
\mathcal O_B(x)_\delta
=
\epsilon_{abc}
(\Gamma_D)_{\alpha\beta}
q_{f_1}(x)_{a\alpha}
q_{f_2}(x)_{b\beta}
q_{f_3}(x)_{c\delta}.
\label{eq:schematic_baryon_operator}
\end{equation}

These equations illustrate the frontend composition rules rather than imposing
one fixed physical choice of \(\Gamma\), \(\Gamma_D\), or flavor content.
SymCorr provides a convenience constructor for the second pattern, but the
result is the same ordinary symbolic operator representation used elsewhere in
the frontend.

Spatially extended operators are built by attaching transformations to the
relevant constituent fields before forming the product. A derivative,
parallel-transport path, shift, or projection therefore remains associated with
the field on which it acts. Likewise, explicit gauge links and color tensors
can appear as factors in the expression when the operator definition requires
them. This allows local and spatially structured operators to share the same
algebraic composition rules.

Products can also combine complete operators. This is the mechanism used to
retain constituent structure in multi-local or multi-hadron constructions. If
\(H_1\) and \(H_2\) are independently defined operators with distinct locality
labels and momentum assignments, their product has the schematic form

\begin{equation}
\mathcal O_{12}
=
H_1(\mathbf p_1)\,H_2(\mathbf p_2),
\qquad
\mathbf P=\mathbf p_1+\mathbf p_2.
\label{eq:composite_hadron_product}
\end{equation}

The symbolic expressions are concatenated and the constituent momentum maps are
merged. Position labels associated with momentum-bearing constituents must be
distinct when such operators are combined; an accidental collision is rejected
rather than silently identifying two locality groups. In this way the
constituent momenta and the total momentum remain simultaneously available to
later stages.

A second form of composition is a formal linear combination of complete basis
operators,

\begin{equation}
\mathcal O
=
\sum_i c_i\,\mathcal O_i .
\label{eq:operator_linear_combination}
\end{equation}

This is represented separately from the internal product structure of each
\(\mathcal O_i\). The frontend supports addition, subtraction, scalar
multiplication, and products of such combinations. Multiplication is
distributive, so the product of two sums generates the corresponding products
of basis operators. Equivalent basis terms are combined by their coefficients,
and terms whose resulting coefficient is numerically zero at the frontend
tolerance are removed.

Hermitian conjugation acts on the full symbolic construction rather than only
on its outer label. For a product, the factor order is reversed and each factor
is conjugated according to its symbolic rule; for a linear combination the
coefficients are complex conjugated and each basis operator is daggered. The
associated momenta reverse sign as discussed above. These operations allow a
source operator to be derived from a sink definition without manually
reconstructing its field ordering, spin structure, or momentum orientation.

Correlators can contain either basis operators or such linear combinations.
The latter are not passed to Wick contraction as an opaque sum. Instead, the
correlator is expanded into weighted basis-operator correlators, after which
Wick contraction is applied to each definite symbolic field product. This keeps
linearity separate from the fermionic pairing problem and ensures that the
coefficients multiplying the final contraction terms remain explicit.

The practical consequence is that meson-like, baryon-like, tetraquark, and
multi-hadron constructions can be assembled from the same small symbolic
vocabulary. Their differences reside in the field content, tensor
connectivity, transformations, momenta, and linear combinations specified by
the operator, not in separate downstream execution interfaces.

\subsection{Simple operator examples}

The following two examples illustrate how the mathematical expressions above
are represented in the current frontend. They are intended only to demonstrate
operator construction; the external-data validation status of particular
channels is discussed separately in Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}.

As a first example, consider the zero-momentum pseudoscalar operator

\begin{equation}
\mathcal O_{\mathrm{PS}}(x)
=
\bar d(x)\,\gamma_5\,u(x).
\label{eq:simple_pseudoscalar_example}
\end{equation}

Using explicit color and spin indices, the corresponding SymCorr definition is

\begin{verbatim}
pion = Operator(
    dbar("x", "a", "alpha")
    * gamma5("alpha", "beta")
    * u("x", "a", "beta"),
    momentum=(0, 0, 0),
)
\end{verbatim}

Here the repeated color label \texttt{a} forms the color singlet, while the
repeated spin labels connect the quark fields through \(\gamma_5\). The
position label \texttt{x} defines one locality group and therefore allows the
single momentum assignment to be attached unambiguously. A corresponding
source operator can be produced from the same definition,

\begin{verbatim}
source = pion.dagger()
corr = Correlator(pion, source)
\end{verbatim}

without inserting a quark propagator or writing the Wick-contracted expression
by hand. Those objects are generated only in the subsequent compilation stages.

For a three-quark example, the repository provides a convenience constructor
for a local baryon-like operator of the form

\begin{equation}
\mathcal O_B(x)_\delta
=
\epsilon_{abc}
(C\gamma_5)_{\alpha\beta}
\ell(x)_{a\alpha}
\ell(x)_{b\beta}
s(x)_{c\delta},
\label{eq:simple_baryon_example}
\end{equation}

where \(\ell\) denotes the generic light-flavor field available in the
frontend. The representative compiler fixture constructs this operator as

\begin{verbatim}
baryon = build_baryon_operator(
    ["l", "l", "s"],
    "Cg5",
    momentum=(0, 0, 0),
)

corr = CorrMatrix([baryon])[0, 0]
\end{verbatim}

The helper inserts the antisymmetric color tensor and the selected diquark spin
matrix but returns the same ordinary symbolic \texttt{Operator} used by the
rest of the frontend. In the one-element correlation matrix shown above, no
explicit source basis is supplied, so SymCorr constructs the source by
Hermitian conjugating the sink operator before the correlator is formed.

The contrast between these two examples is useful for the remainder of the
paper. Increasing the number of quark fields changes the possible Wick
pairings and the eventual contraction topology, but it does not require a new
frontend representation. Both operators enter the compiler as ordered symbolic
field expressions with explicit flavor, index connectivity, spin/color
structure, and momentum information. The automatic Wick-contraction stage is
therefore the first point at which their substantially different propagation
structures emerge.

\section{Automatic Wick Contraction}
\label{sec:wick}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Flavor-compatible contractions}

Wick contraction begins only after any operator linear combinations have been
expanded into definite basis correlators. For one such correlator, SymCorr
collects the quark and antiquark fields from all operator insertions while
retaining their original left-to-right ordering. The first combinatorial task
is then to determine every complete pairing allowed by the explicit quark
flavors carried by those fields.

For each flavor \(f\), let \(Q_f\) denote the set of quark fields and
\(\bar Q_f\) the set of antiquark fields appearing in the correlator. A
complete Wick contraction requires

\begin{equation}
|Q_f| = |\bar Q_f|
\qquad
\text{for every flavor } f.
\label{eq:wick_flavor_balance}
\end{equation}

If this condition fails for any flavor, the current Wick engine produces no
complete contraction term for that basis correlator. Cross-flavor pairings are
never introduced to compensate for the mismatch.

When the flavor counts are balanced, every bijection between the quark and
antiquark fields of the same flavor is generated. If a flavor occurs
\(n_f\) times as a quark and \(n_f\) times as an antiquark, that flavor channel
therefore contributes \(n_f!\) possible pairings. Since the choices for
different flavors are independent, the complete set of flavor-compatible
pairings is the Cartesian product of the channel-wise choices, with total
number

\begin{equation}
N_{\mathrm{pair}}
=
\prod_f n_f! .
\label{eq:number_wick_pairings}
\end{equation}

This enumeration is important once identical flavors occur several times in a
multi-quark or multi-hadron operator: direct and exchange pairings arise from
the same rule rather than being encoded as separate hand-written topologies.

Flavor matching is based on the symbolic QCD flavor, not on the identity of a
numerical propagator resource that may eventually be used at run time. In
particular, \(u\) and \(d\) remain distinct Wick flavors even if a numerical
setup uses the same light-quark parameters or the same underlying data source
for both. Isospin combinations or other flavor superpositions must therefore
be specified explicitly through the operator linear-combination layer; the
Wick engine does not infer them from numerical degeneracies.

The pairing search is also not restricted to fields belonging to different
operator insertions. A quark and antiquark of the same flavor may be paired even
when both occur on the same side of a two-point function or within the same
composite insertion. Retaining these possibilities is necessary for closed
quark loops and disconnected or mixed contraction topologies. Filtering the
pairings to sink--source lines alone would silently remove such terms before
their topology could be identified.

At this stage only the admissible field pairings have been determined. Each
complete pairing is retained explicitly and is passed to the subsequent Wick
steps, which determine its fermionic sign and replace each matched
quark--antiquark pair by the corresponding symbolic propagation line. No
numerical lattice data, contraction ordering, or hardware information enters
this flavor-compatibility decision.

\subsection{Fermionic signs}

The relative sign of each Wick term is fixed by the ordering of the fermionic
fields in the original symbolic correlator. SymCorr therefore retains the
encounter order of all quark-field leaves across the ordered operator
insertions. Spin matrices, color tensors, and spatial transformations remain
part of the operator expression, but they do not themselves contribute to the
Grassmann permutation parity.

Let the ordered fermionic fields in a basis correlator be
\(F=(f_1,\ldots,f_{2N})\), and consider one complete flavor-compatible pairing

\begin{equation}
\mathcal P
=
\bigl\{
(q_{i_1},\bar q_{j_1}),\ldots,
(q_{i_N},\bar q_{j_N})
\bigr\}.
\end{equation}

For the purpose of determining the sign, SymCorr forms the sequence in which
each quark is placed immediately before its paired antiquark,

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathcal P}
=
(q_{i_1},\bar q_{j_1},\ldots,
 q_{i_N},\bar q_{j_N}),
\end{equation}

and determines the parity of the permutation that maps the original field
ordering \(F\) to this paired ordering. If \(I(\mathcal P)\) is the number of
inversions in that permutation, the Wick sign stored by the frontend is

\begin{equation}
s_{\mathcal P}=(-1)^{I(\mathcal P)}.
\label{eq:wick_fermionic_sign}
\end{equation}

This is equivalent to counting the parity of the adjacent exchanges of
fermionic fields required to bring every contracted pair together. The order
in which complete pairs are listed does not affect the result: exchanging two
whole pairs moves two fermionic fields past two others and therefore changes
the permutation by an even number of transpositions.

A four-field example makes the rule explicit. Suppose the original fermionic
order is

\begin{equation}
q_1\,q_2\,\bar q_1\,\bar q_2 .
\end{equation}

For the pairing
\((q_1,\bar q_1)(q_2,\bar q_2)\), the paired sequence corresponds to the
position permutation \((1,3,2,4)\), which contains one inversion and hence has
sign \(-1\). For the exchanged pairing
\((q_1,\bar q_2)(q_2,\bar q_1)\), the position permutation is
\((1,4,2,3)\), which contains two inversions and hence has sign \(+1\).
Thus the relative direct/exchange sign is obtained from the same general
permutation rule used for larger multi-quark systems; it is not inserted as a
special topology-dependent convention.

The sign and the algebraic coefficient are kept as separate pieces of the Wick
term. For a basis correlator the contraction engine initially associates unit
coefficient with each generated pairing and stores its Grassmann parity
separately. If the correlator originated from a linear combination of
operators, the corresponding complex weight multiplies the algebraic
coefficient without altering the sign calculation. At the subsequent
physics-representation boundary, these factors are combined into the
coefficient multiplying the logical correlator,

\begin{equation}
c_{\mathcal P}^{\mathrm{phys}}
=
s_{\mathcal P}\,c_{\mathcal P}.
\label{eq:wick_sign_coefficient}
\end{equation}

Keeping the two quantities distinct during the Wick stage makes the origin of a
minus sign inspectable before later canonicalization, contraction planning, or
numerical execution. In particular, the numerical backend does not infer or
repair fermionic signs from propagator orientation or tensor layout; the sign
is already fixed by the symbolic field ordering.

\subsection{Canonicalization and symbolic simplification}

Canonicalization in SymCorr is used to identify symbolic descriptions that are
mathematically equivalent under rules that are explicit in the implementation.
It is not used to guess additional field-theory identities. In particular, the
frontend retains the expression exactly as supplied by the user, while a
separate structural identity removes dependence on arbitrary dummy-index names.
Spin, color, and position labels are alpha-renamed deterministically, and the
momentum map is transformed through the same position-label mapping. Ordered
factor structure, flavor, transformations, and momentum values remain part of
the operator identity, whereas user metadata is treated as annotation rather
than symbolic physics.

A second canonicalization step occurs at the compiler boundary before Wick
contraction. Field-operation chains are normalized without mutating the
frontend operator. For pure coordinate shifts and gauge-covariant parallel
transport, adjacent inverse lattice hops cancel. For example,

\begin{equation}
(+x,-x,+y)\longrightarrow(+y).
\label{eq:path_canonicalization}
\end{equation}

Nested field operations are then fused into a compiler-internal composite
operation that preserves their order. Other primitive operations are retained
unchanged by this pass, and the operator momentum assignments and metadata are
copied to the normalized compiler-side representation.

This normalization is deliberately performed before linear-combination terms
are merged. Two frontend operators can therefore remain visibly different as
user objects yet become the same compiler basis operator when, for example,
their transport paths differ only by adjacent inverse hops. Once normalized,
weights multiplying identical ordered basis-operator tuples are added. The
current implementation removes a resulting basis weight when its magnitude is
smaller than \(10^{-15}\). For a two-point correlation matrix, the corresponding
simplification pass performs the same coefficient aggregation separately within
each matrix element for identical normalized sink--source basis pairs.

The same normalized operator identity is also used at the Wick-cache boundary.
The cache key is the complete ordered tuple of basis-operator insertions rather
than an unordered set. Consequently, repeated occurrences of the same
normalized symbolic correlator can reuse an already generated Wick expansion,
while a physically different operator ordering remains a distinct contraction
problem. This reuse avoids repeated symbolic work but does not alter the set of
Wick pairings or their fermionic signs.

The scope of these transformations is intentionally restricted. The pre-Wick
normalizer does not commute or reorder fermion factors, replace spin or color
structures using additional identities, infer cyclic trace equivalences, or
simplify expressions using numerical tensor values. Such transformations could
change the field ordering or require assumptions that belong to later compiler
representations. At this stage SymCorr therefore applies only the
canonicalization rules for which equivalence is explicit in the symbolic
algebra.

There is a separate simplification mechanism later in the compilation pipeline,
after the Wick terms have been lowered to numerical contraction graphs. At that
stage exact global common-subexpression analysis can canonicalize reusable
tensor-network components and combine exactly equal complete Wick terms within
a correlator. The later complete-term aggregation uses exact coefficient
cancellation for scalar terms rather than the \(10^{-15}\) tolerance used for
pre-Wick operator-combination weights. Keeping these two stages distinct avoids
conflating symbolic algebra with numerical contraction reuse; the latter is
discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:execution}.

\subsection{Output of the contraction stage}

For each normalized basis correlator, the Wick engine returns an ordered
collection of symbolic Wick terms. A convenient schematic description of one
such term is

\begin{equation}
W_\alpha
=
\left(
 s_\alpha,
 c_\alpha,
 \mathcal P_\alpha,
 \mathcal E_\alpha,
 \mathcal C_\alpha
\right),
\label{eq:wick_term_contents}
\end{equation}

where \(s_\alpha\) is the fermionic sign, \(c_\alpha\) is the algebraic
coefficient, \(\mathcal P_\alpha\) is the explicit set of paired quark and
antiquark fields, \(\mathcal E_\alpha\) is the contracted symbolic expression,
and \(\mathcal C_\alpha\) records the locality-graph components inferred from
the pairing. These quantities remain symbolic: no perambulator, distillation
kernel, tensor shape, external file, memory buffer, or execution device has yet
been introduced.

The pairing \(\mathcal P_\alpha\) is retained directly rather than being
recoverable only from a printed algebraic expression. Each entry identifies the
quark endpoint and the antiquark endpoint of one logical propagation line,
including their flavor and symbolic spin, color, and position labels. This
explicit pairing is the authoritative input used by the next compiler boundary
to construct logical quark lines. It also retains the information needed to
associate field-level derivatives, transports, shifts, and spatial
representations with the correct endpoints.

The symbolic expression \(\mathcal E_\alpha\) retains the non-fermionic tensor
content inherited from the original operators and represents contracted quark
lines symbolically. In particular, spin matrices, color tensors, gauge-link
objects, and their index connectivity remain visible rather than being replaced
by numerical arrays at the Wick stage. The later physics-lowering pass reads
these symbolic tensors and the explicit pairing together; it does not infer the
quark-line topology from numerical tensor shapes.

In addition to the algebraic term, SymCorr computes a locality graph from the
pairing. Position labels form the graph vertices and a contracted quark line
connects the position labels of its two endpoints. Each resulting graph
component records both the locality labels that it contains and the operator
insertions touched by those labels. The present Wick-level topology label is a
coarse diagnostic: a term is classified as \emph{disconnected} when all
components are local to individual operator insertions, \emph{connected} when
there are spanning components and no purely local component, and \emph{mixed}
when spanning and local components occur together. This terminology does not
assert that the complete numerical tensor network consists of one connected
component.

For composite operators, the same component records provide an additional
structural diagnostic. The current \texttt{factorized} property is true when no
locality component spans more than two operator insertions; a component touching
more than two insertions marks a multi-operator exchange structure. This
information is useful for inspecting the generated Wick terms, but it is kept
separate from later numerical common-subexpression analysis and contraction
planning.

When a basis correlator originates from a linear combination, its previously
computed complex basis weight is applied to the Wick-term coefficient while the
cached sign, pairing, expression, and component structure remain unchanged. The
next lowering step then combines sign and coefficient and translates each Wick
term into a backend-independent logical correlator. In the current PhysicsIR,
the resulting records contain logical quark lines, spin tensors, color tensors,
gauge tensors, field-operation information, and momentum assignments. The
Wick-level locality-component and topology diagnostics are not part of this
PhysicsIR data contract.

This boundary is important because it marks the end of the purely symbolic Wick
problem. By the time a term leaves this stage, SymCorr has fixed which fermion
fields contract, the associated Grassmann sign, the algebraic weight, and the
symbolic operator structures that accompany those propagation lines. The next
stage may reorganize this information into backend-independent physics records,
but it is not permitted to invent a new pairing or reinterpret the fermionic
sign.
