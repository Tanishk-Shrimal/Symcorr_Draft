\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The strong coupling of Quantum ChromoDynamics (QCD) at low energies precludes the use of perturbative approaches, where hadrons emerge as the relevant effective degrees of freedom. Consequently, hadron physics investigations in the low energy regime rely on nonperturbative approaches such as those using the lattice QCD framework. Generation of lattice QCD ensembles through Monte-Carlo simulations and estimation of fermion matrix determinant as well as inversions of the fermion matrix consume substantial fraction of the computer time in lattice QCD calculation and have been extensively discussed and investigated since the inception of lattice QCD \cite{}. Physical observables in hadron-physics investigations are extracted from Euclidean correlation functions. In the early years of lattice QCD, these calculations primarily targeted ground-state hadron properties and were comparatively inexpensive. The discovery and study of numerous heavy and excited hadrons over the past several decades, however, necessitated increasingly sophisticated spectroscopy calculations, notably those employing Euclidean correlation matrices and variational analyses to determine the finite-volume spectrum and its properties. As the size and complexity of these correlation matrices increase, the associated operator construction, Wick contractions, and numerical evaluation become increasingly demanding, resulting in substantial rise in computational costs.

Modern day hadron spectroscopy proceeds through evaluation of two point Euclidean correlation matrices 
\beq 
C_{ij}(t-t_0)=\left\langle\mathcal O_i(t)\,\mathcal O_j^{\dagger}(t_0)\right\rangle
\eeq{2ptCfn}
for a basis of interpolators/operators $\{O_i\}$ that carry the quantum numbers of the desired hadronic state, where $t$ and $t_0$ refers to the sink and the source timeslices. In this context, the new challenges include a systematic construction of interpolating operators that enter the evaluation of correlation matrices, handling the allowed Wick contractions for every combination of interpolating operators, keeping track of the fermionic signs with permutations, flavor, spin, color and spatial indices faithfully, etc. The amount of bookkeeping therefore grows as one moves from meson operators to baryons, multi-hadron operators and nuclei. Additional conversions and reductions need to be made to express the correlation matrices in terms of the objects defining operator structures as well as the fermion matrix inverses. Note that different matrix elements carry similarities and share quark-propagation data, operator-dependent building blocks, and often portions of the resulting contraction expressions. This observation suggests opportunities to identify repeated (sub)objects IO and computations, reducing the computational redundancy. While a systematic road-map has already been laid out for such excited hadron spectroscopic investigations in the literature, performing them manually for every new channel being studied requires substantial implementation effort and can introduce bookkeeping errors.

There already exists tools to handle the operator construction and correlation matrix evaluation. Tools such as Multi-Hadron Interpolators (MHI)~\cite{PhysRevD.109.094516} and \texttt{OpTion}~\cite{Yan:2025op} provides a systematic construction of multi-hadron lattice operators projected to finite-volume irreps, total lab frame momentum, spin, and internal quantum numbers. The QCT package \cite{Djukanovic:2016spv} generates \texttt{C++} codes compatible with \texttt{QDP++} \cite{Edwards:2004sx} to perform Wick contractions for a given set of hadronic currents/operators expressed in terms of (anti)quark fields, without further optimization for computational efficiency. The Redstar framework ~\cite{10.1145/3592979.3593409} expresses each element in correlation matrices as a sum of graphs (Wick contractions) and utilizes uses graph-contraction algorithms to reduce redundant evaluations and to organize optimized correlator evaluations in a distillation-based calculational setup \cite{Peardon:2009gh} using tensor contractions for execution on CPUs and GPUs. Another recent algorithmic work explored determinant representations of Wick contractions and methods for identifying smaller sets of determinant terms that are sufficient for evaluating nuclear correlation functions~\cite{PhysRevD.110.114505}.

The emphasis of the \textsc{SymCorr} package is on having a complete automated transformation from a symbolic quark-level operator description to a numerical calculation within the distillation prescription~\cite{Peardon:2009gh} as a sequence of explicit compilation stages. Once the user provides the symbolic definition of a basis of operators in terms of quark and gauge fields, the operator representations, Wick contractions, automated translation to backend-independent physics objects, distillation lowering, execution planning, and numerical evaluation are organized as distinct layers of the software. This staged organization reduces manual intervention in correlator-specific algebra and implementation, while maintaining a clear separation between the physical definition of an operator and the details of its numerical evaluation. It also makes the intermediate algebraic transformations explicit and amenable to inspection and validation, while allowing numerical execution strategies to evolve and adapt independent of the underlying physics description of the interpolating operators.

\section{Overview and scope of \textsc{SymCorr}}
\label{sec:background}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\textsc{SymCorr} package facilitates the computation of Euclidean correlation matrices in the distillation framework, serving itself as a generalized automated platform in computation of arbitrary multi-hadron correlation matrices in finite-volume, with the assumption that the relevant lattice numerical data and the operator basis details are provided by the user. We begin this section by briefly discussing numerical objects relevant for the distillation framework for completeness. In the conventional non-distillation setup, the measurements involve computation of correlation matrices given in Eq. \ref{eq:2ptCfn} for a given operator basis and varying values of $t$ and $t_0$. These involve appropriate tensor index contractions between spatial-color-spin indices of the hadron interpolators with those in the quark line objects referred to as the quark propagators. 

The computational cost of such tensor index contractions spanning through the space-color-spin index values ($\mathcal{O}(10^6)$ for a $40^3$ spatial lattice) can become prohibitively expensive when then system being studied is large or complex such as multi-hadron operators, tetraquarks, pentaquarks, nuclei, etc. In the distillation framework, the combined space-color index is replaced by a single index, which could run over a appropriately chosen eigenmode subset of discretized gauge-covariant Laplacian $\{v^{(i)}(t)\}$. Such a replacement can be facilitated through quark smearing program defined in terms of $\{v^{(i)}(t)\}$ as 
\begin{align}
\tilde q(t)=\Box(t)q(t), &\qquad   
\tilde{\bar q}(t)=\bar q(t)\Box(t), \nonumber \\
\mbox{~~where ~~~} \Box(t)&=V(t)V^\dagger(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N_v}|v^{(i)}><v^{(i)}|(t),  \nonumber \\ 
\mbox{~and ~~~} V(t)&=\left(v^{(1)}(t),v^{(2)}(t),\ldots,v^{(N_v)}(t)\right).
\end{align}
The $\Box(t)$ operator acts as a low pass filter or a projection operator, with the lowest $N_v$ modes. Inserting such projection operators for the quark fields in the conventional correlations, followed regrouping the objects to form new objects such as 
\begin{align}
\tau_f(t,t_0) &= V^\dagger(t) M_f^{-1}(t,t_0) V(t_0) \\ 
\Phi_{\mathcal{O}}(t)&= V^\dagger(t)\,\mathcal{O}\,V(t).
\end{align}
referred to as the perambulators and elementals/kernels, which takes over the information from the quark propagator $M_f^{-1}(t,t_0)$ and hadronic operator $\Phi_A(t)$, respectively. The objects $\tau_f(t,t_0)$ and $\Phi_{\mathcal{O}}$ carry indices running of the $N_v\sim100-1000$ modes considered facilitating contractions of tensor indices with reduced computational cost. This novel procedure was not only found to reduce the computational cost requirement involved in tensor contractions, but also found to provide access to all-to-all quark propagators, to source momentum projection in hadron operator, multi-hadron operators, etc. The expensive quark-propagation information can be prepared independently of a particular spectroscopy operator basis, while changes to momenta, spin structures, or spatial constructions modify the operator-dependent factors. Within the distillation framework, the correlation matrix elements can be written in a compact form as 
\begin{equation}
C_{ij}(t,t_0)= \sum_{\alpha} c_{\alpha}\,\operatorname{Contr}_{\alpha}\left[\{\Phi\},\{\tau\}\right],
\label{eq:distillation_contraction_schematic}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ runs over the Wick-contractions ``$\operatorname{Contr}_{\alpha}\left[...\right]$'' and $c_{\alpha}$ carries the
associated numerical coefficient and the fermionic sign. 

The \textsc{SymCorr} package is designed to separate the physical definition and representation of correlation measurements from the details of their numerical evaluation. The user-provided interpolators determine the Wick contractions and their representation in the distillation space, as illustrated schematically in Eq.~\ref{eq:distillation_contraction_schematic}, while the subsequent numerical procedures, including tensor contractions, redundancy optimizations to avoid repetitive evaluations, memory (de)allocation, and data reuse, are handled by the \textsc{SymCorr} backend. The physics-level description of the measurements are preserved at every stage of the \textsc{SymCorr} flowchart to facilitate user-end as well as backend verifications and troubleshooting, while the numerical tensor objects and operations remain blind to the physics that is aimed at. The correlation matrix evaluation is treated within the \textsc{SymCorr} package as a single structured computational problem,  instead of compiling every matrix element as an independent numerical program. This helps in identifying similar algebraic expressions as well as redundant mathematical work across the full calculation and thus providing efficient choices for tensor contraction ordering and data reuse, enabling an optimized workflow. Following the optimization aprogram t the algebraic as well numerical level, \textsc{SymCorr} also makes educated decisions at the execution stage in regrouping calculations to utilize the provided the hardware resources to its maximum potential. All optimizations, at the algebraic, numerical, and execution stages, are handled by the \textsc{SymCorr} backend while preserving the underlying physics information. This shields non-expert users from the complexities of optimization, while still giving expert users full freedom to inspect the practical implementation and numerical performance.

We present the \textsc{SymCorr} workflow in Fig.~\ref{fig:symcorr_workflow}. The user provides the interpolator basis in symbolic form (Sec.~\ref{sec:symbolic}) together with a job specification that fixes the lattice dimensions, the number of distillation modes, the spin basis, the data locations, and the source and sink times. The calculation then proceeds in two stages. The compile stage uses only the symbolic operators and the compile settings. Linear combinations of operators are expanded into basis correlators, and all quark-line pairings (Wick contractions), their fermionic signs, and their coefficients are generated for every element of the correlation matrix (Sec.~\ref{sec:wick}). The resulting expressions are lowered to the distillation framework, where each Wick term becomes a tensor network of operator-dependent spatial kernels, propagation (perambulator) resources, and spin tensors (Sec.~\ref{sec:distillation_lowering}). All tensor networks of the requested correlation matrix are then treated as one global contraction problem: identical resources and identical contraction networks are shared across Wick terms and matrix elements, exactly equal terms are combined, a contraction order is chosen, shared intermediates are identified, and the operations are scheduled together with a plan for reusing memory buffers (Sec.~\ref{sec:execution}). The result is saved as a portable plan that depends neither on lattice data nor on hardware. In the run stage, the saved plan is first checked against the current operator definitions and job specification. \textsc{SymCorr} then chooses a CPU or GPU deployment and a memory-compatible batching of time slices for the available hardware, reads the Laplacian eigenvectors $\{v^{(i)}(t)\}$, the perambulators $\tau_f(t,t_0)$ and, for derivative or displaced operators, the gauge configuration in the form of the stout-smeared spatial links used to construct the eigenvectors, builds the required kernels and propagation tensors, and executes the frozen plan for every gauge configuration and time context. The correlation functions are written to an HDF5 file; a record of the run and a human-readable report can be produced on request. 

Now we present the scope of the \textsc{SymCorr} package. The present implementation can serve in optimized distillation-based correlation matrix evaluations and works on both CPU as well as GPU resources. The package assumes externally supplied operator basis, lattice QCD configuration, associated Laplacian eigenvectors, and the quark perambulators. Operator constructions such as those dealt by packages (MHI) and \texttt{OpTion} supplied n Ref. \cite{PhysRevD.109.094516,Yan:2025op}, generation of the lattice QCD configurations \cite{openQCD, MILC} and evaluation of Laplacian eigenvectors, and perambulators \cite{quada_laph} are outside \textsc{SymCorr}'s scope. The package can inspect the available resources such as CPUs, GPUs, memory, and can make educated decisions on efficient execution, however it does not support multi-GPU and MPI based multi-CPU evaluations, which is a straightforward extension. These are limited execution boundaries rather than restrictions on the symbolic definition of the correlator.

At the physics level, the \textsc{SymCorr} package has been validated for a variety of local as well as bilocal hadron operators, and their full distillation two point correlation matrix evaluations. The results has been cross-verified with independent Chroma based evaluations for rest as well as (multiple) moving frames, for single mesons, single baryons as well as bilocal two-hadron interpolators.  Correlation matrices with local four-quark operator and relavant bilocal two meson operators in the $T_{1g}$ have also been tested and cross-verified with an independent realization in the Croma software suite. Beyond this explicitly verified cases, the package has additional capabilities, that includes, but not limited to, multi-hadron correlation matrix evaluations, derivative-based single hadron correlator evaluations, correlation matrices involving disconnected quark loops, three point correlation matrices provided the generalized perambulators \cite{}.

\section{Symbolic Representation of Interpolating Operators}
\label{sec:symbolic}

The \textsc{SymCorr} package takes in the interpolator basis as a list of ordered algebraic expressions built out of objects representing (anti)quark fields, gauge fields, spin and color structures. At this stage of interpolator definition, the representation is independent of distillation tensors, data layouts, contraction paths, and execution backends. This strategy helps in retaining the physical structure at all stages of the execution. 

Quark and antiquark fields of flavor $f$ are represented schematically as
\begin{equation}
q_f(x)_{a\alpha},
\qquad
\bar q_f(x)_{a\alpha},
\label{eq:symbolic_quark_field}
\end{equation}
where $x$, $a$, and $\alpha$ denote position, color, and Dirac spin indices, respectively. The flavor and the distinction between a quark and an antiquark are set as the properties of the fermion field objects to facilitate easy identification of flavor compatibility and fermion permutation signs at the stage of Wick contractions. 

The operator representations are independent of the  Dirac gamma matrix conventions


Spin structure is represented by indexed matrices $\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}$, whose numerical convention is only assumed only at the backend.

The frontend provides the usual gamma matrices and
also permits named spin matrices used by the supported operator constructions,
including charge-conjugation structures. At the symbolic level the important
information is the matrix identity together with its two spin indices; the
concrete numerical representation of the spin matrices is a later convention
and does not alter the operator expression.

Color contractions are explicit symbolic tensors. The current frontend contains
both the Kronecker tensor \(\delta_{ab}\) and the antisymmetric tensor
\(\epsilon_{abc}\), which provide the basic color structures required by the
implemented meson- and baryon-like constructions. Gauge transport can also be
represented explicitly through a gauge-link object
\(U(x,y)_{ab}\) carrying its endpoints and open color indices. Keeping these
objects visible in the expression prevents color connectivity from being
inferred later from the ordering or shape of numerical arrays.

Spatial transformations acting on individual fields are represented separately
from the fields themselves. The current frontend distinguishes a pure
coordinate shift from gauge-covariant parallel transport, provides a symbolic
covariant derivative along a directed lattice axis, and supports field-level
spatial projections such as the LapH and point representations. These
transformations are attached to a particular field expression rather than being
silently absorbed into the field. Consequently, an object such as
\(D_\mu q_f\) retains the fact that a derivative acts on that specific field as
the expression proceeds through the compiler.

Products of the elementary objects form larger symbolic expressions, with the
factor ordering retained explicitly. This is essential for fermionic
expressions because the ordering of quark fields enters the sign obtained after
Wick contraction. A complete interpolating operator then wraps such an
expression together with its momentum assignment or assignments and optional
metadata. For operators with several spatial locality groups, momentum can be
associated with the corresponding position labels rather than being forced into
one global momentum value.

A quark propagator is deliberately not part of the user-authored symbolic
vocabulary. Propagator objects arise only after the source and sink expressions
have been combined and Wick contraction has paired compatible quark and
antiquark fields. This boundary ensures that the user specifies the
interpolating operator itself, while propagation topology is derived from the
field content rather than supplied manually.

\subsection{Indices and quantum numbers}

SymCorr assigns every symbolic index to one of three spaces: spin, color, or
position. An index is therefore not identified by its text label alone. When a
label is first introduced, its index space is fixed; attempting to reuse the
same label as a different type is rejected. Reusing the label within the same
space refers to the same symbolic index and hence defines the intended tensor
connectivity of the operator.

Spin and color indices follow the usual Einstein-style interpretation within a
symbolic expression. An index appearing twice denotes a contracted index, while
a single occurrence remains open. Open spin indices are permitted because, for
example, baryon operators can carry an uncontracted Dirac index. An uncontracted
color index is allowed by the representation but is reported as a warning
because the resulting operator may not be gauge invariant. Spin or color
indices appearing more than twice are rejected rather than assigned an
ambiguous contraction meaning.

Position indices play a different role. They identify the spatial or spacetime
locality associated with fields and gauge structures and are therefore not
subject to the same multiplicity rule. Two fields carrying the same position
label belong to the same locality group, whereas distinct labels can be used to
represent bilocal or more general composite operators. In this sense the
position labels participate in the physical specification of the operator even
though their literal names are dummy labels.

The frontend does not combine all quantum numbers into a single opaque tag.
Flavor is stored directly on each quark field, spin and color information are
encoded by the indexed fields and tensors, and momentum is attached to the
operator through its position-locality groups. If an operator contains exactly
one such group, a single three-component lattice momentum is unambiguous. If it
contains several independent locality groups, the momentum assignment is given
as a map from the corresponding position labels to three-component momenta,

\begin{equation}
 x_r \longmapsto \mathbf p_r,
 \qquad
 \mathbf P = \sum_r \mathbf p_r ,
\label{eq:operator_momentum_groups}
\end{equation}

where \(\mathbf P\) is the total momentum carried by the composite operator.
This representation keeps the momentum associated with each constituent
locality visible rather than replacing it immediately by the total momentum.
Under Hermitian conjugation, every stored momentum assignment changes sign,
consistent with the reversed source--sink orientation.

The literal names chosen for dummy spin, color, and position indices are not
part of the mathematical identity of an operator. SymCorr compares operator
structure after a deterministic relabeling of these indices while preserving
the ordered expression and the associated momentum assignments. Thus two
operators that differ only by dummy-index names can be recognized as the same
symbolic structure, whereas a change of flavor, tensor connectivity, factor
ordering, spatial transformation, or momentum remains physically significant.

A second use of relabeling occurs when several operators are inserted into one
correlator. Each insertion is placed in a distinct index namespace before Wick
contraction. The corresponding position labels in the momentum map are renamed
at the same time. This prevents a source index and a sink index from becoming
accidentally identified merely because the two independently constructed
operators happened to use the same dummy label. Intentional contractions
inside each operator remain unchanged.

For a two-point correlation matrix, the sink and source bases are retained as
separate ordered objects. When an explicit source basis is not supplied, the
source operators are constructed as the Hermitian conjugates of the sink
operators; otherwise the two bases may be specified independently. This keeps
source--sink orientation, flavor content, momentum, and open-index structure
explicit before the Wick-contraction stage determines the allowed propagation
lines.

\subsection{Constructing composite operators}

Composite operators are constructed by multiplying the elementary symbolic
objects introduced above while preserving their order and typed-index
connectivity. This gives one common representation for operator structures of
different hadronic complexity rather than requiring a separate symbolic
language for each hadron family. Schematically, a local meson-like operator may
have the form

\begin{equation}
\mathcal O_M(x)
=
\bar q_{f_1}(x)_{a\alpha}
\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}
q_{f_2}(x)_{a\beta},
\label{eq:schematic_meson_operator}
\end{equation}

whereas a local three-quark structure can be written as

\begin{equation}
\mathcal O_B(x)_\delta
=
\epsilon_{abc}
(\Gamma_D)_{\alpha\beta}
q_{f_1}(x)_{a\alpha}
q_{f_2}(x)_{b\beta}
q_{f_3}(x)_{c\delta}.
\label{eq:schematic_baryon_operator}
\end{equation}

These equations illustrate the frontend composition rules rather than imposing
one fixed physical choice of \(\Gamma\), \(\Gamma_D\), or flavor content.
SymCorr provides a convenience constructor for the second pattern, but the
result is the same ordinary symbolic operator representation used elsewhere in
the frontend.

Spatially extended operators are built by attaching transformations to the
relevant constituent fields before forming the product. A derivative,
parallel-transport path, shift, or projection therefore remains associated with
the field on which it acts. Likewise, explicit gauge links and color tensors
can appear as factors in the expression when the operator definition requires
them. This allows local and spatially structured operators to share the same
algebraic composition rules.

Products can also combine complete operators. This is the mechanism used to
retain constituent structure in multi-local or multi-hadron constructions. If
\(H_1\) and \(H_2\) are independently defined operators with distinct locality
labels and momentum assignments, their product has the schematic form

\begin{equation}
\mathcal O_{12}
=
H_1(\mathbf p_1)\,H_2(\mathbf p_2),
\qquad
\mathbf P=\mathbf p_1+\mathbf p_2.
\label{eq:composite_hadron_product}
\end{equation}

The symbolic expressions are concatenated and the constituent momentum maps are
merged. Position labels associated with momentum-bearing constituents must be
distinct when such operators are combined; an accidental collision is rejected
rather than silently identifying two locality groups. In this way the
constituent momenta and the total momentum remain simultaneously available to
later stages.

A second form of composition is a formal linear combination of complete basis
operators,

\begin{equation}
\mathcal O
=
\sum_i c_i\,\mathcal O_i .
\label{eq:operator_linear_combination}
\end{equation}

This is represented separately from the internal product structure of each
\(\mathcal O_i\). The frontend supports addition, subtraction, scalar
multiplication, and products of such combinations. Multiplication is
distributive, so the product of two sums generates the corresponding products
of basis operators. Equivalent basis terms are combined by their coefficients,
and terms whose resulting coefficient is numerically zero at the frontend
tolerance are removed.

Hermitian conjugation acts on the full symbolic construction rather than only
on its outer label. For a product, the factor order is reversed and each factor
is conjugated according to its symbolic rule; for a linear combination the
coefficients are complex conjugated and each basis operator is daggered. The
associated momenta reverse sign as discussed above. These operations allow a
source operator to be derived from a sink definition without manually
reconstructing its field ordering, spin structure, or momentum orientation.

Correlators can contain either basis operators or such linear combinations.
The latter are not passed to Wick contraction as an opaque sum. Instead, the
correlator is expanded into weighted basis-operator correlators, after which
Wick contraction is applied to each definite symbolic field product. This keeps
linearity separate from the fermionic pairing problem and ensures that the
coefficients multiplying the final contraction terms remain explicit.

The practical consequence is that meson-like, baryon-like, tetraquark, and
multi-hadron constructions can be assembled from the same small symbolic
vocabulary. Their differences reside in the field content, tensor
connectivity, transformations, momenta, and linear combinations specified by
the operator, not in separate downstream execution interfaces.

\subsection{Simple operator examples}

The following two examples illustrate how the mathematical expressions above
are represented in the current frontend. They are intended only to demonstrate
operator construction; the external-data validation status of particular
channels is discussed separately in Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}.

As a first example, consider the zero-momentum pseudoscalar operator

\begin{equation}
\mathcal O_{\mathrm{PS}}(x)
=
\bar d(x)\,\gamma_5\,u(x).
\label{eq:simple_pseudoscalar_example}
\end{equation}

\section{Automatic Wick Contraction}
\label{sec:wick}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The correlators defined in Sec.~\ref{sec:symbolic} enter the first step of the compile stage in
Fig.~\ref{fig:symcorr_workflow}, which turns each of them into a sum of quark-line contractions.
Wick contraction is applied to basis correlators. A correlator containing linear combinations of
operators is first expanded into weighted basis correlators, and the weights multiply the
resulting terms; identical basis correlators are contracted only once.

\subsection{Flavor-compatible contractions}

For one basis correlator, \textsc{SymCorr} collects the quark and antiquark fields of all operator
insertions in their original order and generates every complete pairing that conserves the flavor
carried by each field. If a flavor $f$ occurs $n_f$ times as a quark and $n_f$ times as an
antiquark, every bijection between them is generated, so that the number of pairings is
\begin{equation}
N_{\mathrm{pair}}=\prod_f n_f!\,.
\label{eq:number_wick_pairings}
\end{equation}
If the counts differ for any flavor, the correlator has no complete contraction. Flavor is the
symbolic QCD flavor: $u$ and $d$ remain distinct even when the same perambulator data are used for
both, and isospin combinations are written explicitly as linear combinations of operators. Pairings
are not restricted to fields in different insertions; a quark and an antiquark on the same time
slice may be contracted, which produces equal-time lines and closed quark loops
(Sec.~\ref{sec:topologies}).

\subsection{Fermionic signs}

The relative sign of a Wick term is fixed by the ordering of the fermion fields in the correlator;
spin matrices, color tensors and field operations do not affect it.
For the ordered fields $F=(f_1,\ldots,f_{2N})$ and one complete pairing
$\mathcal P=\{(q_{i_1},\bar q_{j_1}),\ldots,(q_{i_N},\bar q_{j_N})\}$, \textsc{SymCorr} forms the
sequence $F_{\mathcal P}=(q_{i_1},\bar q_{j_1},\ldots,q_{i_N},\bar q_{j_N})$ and assigns
\begin{equation}
s_{\mathcal P}=(-1)^{I(\mathcal P)},
\label{eq:wick_fermionic_sign}
\end{equation}
where $I(\mathcal P)$ is the number of inversions of the permutation that maps $F$ to
$F_{\mathcal P}$. The order in which the pairs are listed does not matter, because exchanging two
complete pairs is an even permutation. For the field order $q_1\,q_2\,\bar q_1\,\bar q_2$, the
pairing $(q_1,\bar q_1)(q_2,\bar q_2)$ corresponds to the permutation $(1,3,2,4)$ with one inversion
and sign $-1$, and the exchanged pairing $(q_1,\bar q_2)(q_2,\bar q_1)$ to $(1,4,2,3)$ with two
inversions and sign $+1$. Direct and exchange signs of all multi-quark systems follow from the same
rule. The sign is combined with the expansion weight into the coefficient of the term; the numerical
backend never infers or repairs signs.

\subsection{Canonicalization}

Before contraction, chains of field operations are normalized, for example by cancelling adjacent
inverse hops of shifts and transports, $(+x,-x,+y)\to(+y)$. Basis operators that differ only in the
names of contracted indices are identified and their coefficients added; terms whose coefficients
cancel exactly are removed. Open indices keep their names, so that, e.g., an operator and its
transpose in two open spin indices are not identified. No further identities, such as Fierz
rearrangements or relations that depend on numerical values, are applied.

\subsection{Contraction topologies}
\label{sec:topologies}

Because the pairing search is complete within each flavor and is not restricted to source--sink
lines, every quark-flow topology allowed by the flavor content of the operators is generated
automatically; no diagram is entered by hand or selected by the user. This includes quark lines
that start and end on the same time slice and closed quark loops. Representative diagrams are
shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:topologies}, and Table~\ref{tab:topology_counts} lists the number of Wick
terms that \textsc{SymCorr} generates for representative operators, separated into terms whose
quark lines all connect source and sink, terms that contain equal-time lines, and disconnected
terms with closed loops. The relative signs and the subsequent tensor-network construction follow
from the same rules for all of them. With this step the pairings, signs and coefficients are fixed;
the following stages only decide how the resulting terms are evaluated.

\section{Lowering to the Distillation Representation}
\label{sec:distillation_lowering}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

After Wick contraction every term is a product of quark propagators and of the spin, color and
spatial structures of the operators. In distillation, the propagators are replaced by perambulators
and the operator structure on each time slice by spatial kernels built from the Laplacian
eigenvectors. \textsc{SymCorr} derives both from the explicit index connectivity of the operators,
without a library of meson, baryon or tetraquark shapes. This second compile step
(Fig.~\ref{fig:symcorr_workflow}) is specific to distillation but still independent of the lattice
data: it determines which kernels and perambulators are needed, not their values.

\subsection{Operator kernels}

With the smearing of Sec.~\ref{sec:background}, a quark field contributes $V$ and an antiquark
field $V^\dagger$. The fields of one
time slice are grouped into components that are connected by shared positions, color indices or
color tensors; each component becomes one spatial kernel. For a component $A$ with positions
$\mathbf x_r$, momenta $\mathbf p_r$ and external LapH-mode indices $k_i$,
\begin{align}
\mathcal K_A(\{k_i\};t)
&=
\sum_{\{\mathbf x_r\},\{a_i\}}
e^{\,i\sum_r \mathbf p_r\cdot\mathbf x_r}\,
\mathcal C_A(\{a_i\})\nonumber\\
&\qquad\times\prod_i
\mathcal V_i(\mathbf x_{r(i)},a_i,k_i;t),
\label{eq:generic_operator_spatial_kernel}
\end{align}
where $\mathcal C_A$ is the color tensor and $\mathcal V_i$ the eigenvector factor of field $i$,
complex conjugated for an antiquark, after the derivative, transport or shift operations of that
field (Eq.~\eqref{eq:transport_conventions}) have been applied. The spatial sum carries no volume
normalization. A local color-singlet bilinear and a local three-quark operator give
\begin{align}
K_{kl}^{(\mathbf p)}(t)
&=\sum_{\mathbf x,a} e^{\,i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}
 \left[\mathcal D_{\bar q}V(t)\right]^*_{\mathbf x a k}
 \left[\mathcal D_q V(t)\right]_{\mathbf x a l},
\label{eq:bilinear_spatial_kernel}\\
B_{k_1k_2k_3}^{(\mathbf p)}(t)
&=\sum_{\mathbf x,a,b,c} e^{\,i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}\,\epsilon_{abc}
 \left[\mathcal D_1 V(t)\right]_{\mathbf x a k_1}\nonumber\\
&\qquad\times\left[\mathcal D_2 V(t)\right]_{\mathbf x b k_2}
 \left[\mathcal D_3 V(t)\right]_{\mathbf x c k_3},
\label{eq:three_quark_spatial_kernel}
\end{align}
where $\mathcal D$ denotes the field operations. Spin matrices are not folded into the kernels; they
remain separate tensors. As a consequence, operators that differ only in their spin structure, such as
$\bar d\gamma_5 u$ and $\bar d\gamma_i u$, or nucleon operators with different diquark matrices,
share the same kernel. Kernels with the same field orientations, field operations, color structure
and momenta are identified as one resource, independently of the names of their indices and of the
quark flavors, since the eigenvectors do not depend on flavor.

\subsection{Propagation}

An open quark line from a source kernel to a sink kernel becomes a perambulator of the line's
flavor with the index order
\begin{equation}
\tau_f(t_{\mathrm{snk}},t_{\mathrm{src}})_{k\alpha,\,l\beta},
\label{eq:propagation_resource_layout}
\end{equation}
where $(k,\alpha)$ are the sink mode and spin and $(l,\beta)$ the source mode and spin. If only
perambulators from a fixed source time $t_0$ are stored, the reverse direction is reconstructed with
$\gamma_5$-Hermiticity,
\begin{equation}
\tau(t_0,t)=\gamma_5\,\tau(t,t_0)^\dagger\,\gamma_5 ,
\label{eq:fixed_source_reverse_propagation}
\end{equation}
where the adjoint acts on both mode and spin indices; a request in which neither time is the fixed
source is rejected. Storage conventions of external files, such as a stored $\bar q\gamma_4$ source
field, are converted once when the tensor is read. A closed quark loop is evaluated from
perambulators with equal source and sink times, $\tau_f(t,t)$, connected through the kernels and spin
matrices of the loop; this requires perambulators from all source times.

An open quark line may also pass through a local current at an intermediate time, as in a
three-point function. If the fields of the current are written in point representation, the line
becomes a generalized perambulator $\mathcal T^{J}_{f_0\to f_1}(t_2,t_1,t_0)$, which also allows a
change of flavor at the current and is supplied by the user's data provider. This path is
implemented but has not yet been validated against external data (Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}).

Kernels and perambulators refer to the operator insertions of the correlator (sink, source and any
current), not to numerical times. The source and sink times, like the gauge configurations, are
listed in the job specification and attached only at run time, so that one compiled plan serves the
complete grid of times and configurations.

\subsection{Tensor network of a Wick term}

Each Wick term $\alpha$ is converted into a tensor network of three kinds of tensors, the kernels,
the perambulators and the spin matrices,
\begin{equation}
C_\alpha
=
c_\alpha
\sum_{\{i\}_{\mathrm{internal}}}
\prod_n
T_{\alpha n}(I_{\alpha n}),
\label{eq:primitive_contraction_task}
\end{equation}
where $c_\alpha$ contains the fermionic sign and the expansion weight, $T_{\alpha n}$ are the tensors
and $I_{\alpha n}$ their indices. An index shared by two tensors is summed, and an index that occurs
once remains open, as for the spin index of a baryon correlator. The network fixes which tensors are
contracted with which, but not the order of the contractions; that is chosen globally in
Sec.~\ref{sec:execution}.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Global Optimization and Execution}
\label{sec:execution}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The tensor networks of all Wick terms and all elements of the requested correlation matrix are
treated as one contraction problem, which is optimized in four steps: (i)~identical tensors and
identical networks are shared (Sec.~\ref{sec:reuse_repeated_structures}); (ii)~a pairwise
contraction order is chosen for every distinct network, currently with \texttt{opt\_einsum}, using
the index dimensions
\begin{equation}
|i|=
\begin{cases}
N_v, & i\in\text{LapH-mode space},\\
4,   & i\in\text{Dirac-spin space};
\end{cases}
\label{eq:execution_index_size_model}
\end{equation}
(iii)~partial contractions that occur in several networks are computed once; and (iv)~the
operations are scheduled together with a plan for the memory buffers
(Sec.~\ref{sec:memory_execution}). These are the last two steps of the compile stage in
Fig.~\ref{fig:symcorr_workflow}. Everything is fixed in the saved plan before any lattice data are
read, and the plan records the estimated operation count and peak memory, which can be inspected
before a calculation is started.

\label{sec:reuse_repeated_structures}

\begin{equation}
\mathcal O_N(t) = \epsilon_{abc} \left[ u^a(t)^\top C\gamma_5 d^b(t) \right] P_+ u^c(t),
\label{eq:proton_interpolator_def}
\end{equation}

The same tensor network appears with different node orders and index names in different terms and
correlators. To recognize it, \textsc{SymCorr} assigns every connected network a canonical form
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:canonical_graph_isomorphism}): starting from each tensor in turn, it walks the
network by following the tensor ports in a fixed order, renames the contracted indices
$i_0,i_1,\ldots$ and the open indices $o_0,o_1,\ldots$ in the order in which they are reached, and
encodes the tensor kinds, resources and connections into a string. The lexicographically smallest
string over all starting tensors is the canonical form, and its hash identifies the network. Two
networks receive the same identifier exactly when they are identical up to the order of their tensors
and the names of their contracted indices; the full string is compared whenever two hashes agree.
Contraction graphs of hadron correlators are small, typically 5--25 tensors, so this exhaustive
search is cheap and needs no external graph library. Identical complete terms within one correlator
are combined, and terms whose coefficients cancel are removed before any contraction is scheduled.

Reuse occurs at three levels. Tensors are shared across all terms and matrix elements: for a
$2\times2$ nucleon correlation matrix built from the operator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:proton_interpolator_def}
and its analogue with the diquark matrix $C\gamma_4$, the eight Wick terms contain 96 tensors but only
11 distinct ones (two kernels, the $u$ and $d$ perambulators and seven spin matrices). Identical
networks are evaluated once; in this example all eight networks differ, because the diquark
matrices differ. Finally, partial contractions are shared between different networks: for the
two-nucleon correlator of Table~\ref{tab:topology_counts} at $N_v=64$, the contraction trees of the 48
Wick terms contain 1008 partial contractions, of which 260 are distinct, which removes about one
third of the floating-point operations. The reuse for the systems studied in this work is
quantified in Sec.~\ref{sec:performance}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Canonical Graph Isomorphism in \textsc{SymCorr}}
\label{alg:canonical_graph_isomorphism}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Contraction graph \(G\) with tensor nodes, port definitions, and index connections.
\Ensure Canonical identifier \texttt{computation\_id} and standardized contraction plan.
\State \textbf{Initialize} candidate list \(\mathcal C \gets [\,]\).
\For{each candidate starting tensor \(r\) in \(G\)}
    \State Walk through connected tensors starting from \(r\), following port connections in fixed numerical port order.
    \State Record the sequential visit order of tensors \((t_0, t_1, \ldots)\).
    \State Encode tensor kinds, underlying resource IDs, and relative edge steps into a standardized string \(S_r\).
    \State Add \(S_r\) to candidate list \(\mathcal C\).
\EndFor
\State \textbf{Select} the alphabetically earliest candidate:
\[ S_{\mathrm{min}} \gets \min(\mathcal C) \]
\State \textbf{Standardize indices}: Rename internal contracted indices to \(\{i_0, i_1, i_2, \dots\}\) and open indices to \(\{o_0, o_1, \dots\}\) according to the winning walk.
\State \textbf{Generate fingerprint}: Compute the cryptographic hash:
\[ \texttt{computation\_id} \gets \operatorname{SHA-256}(S_{\mathrm{min}})[:20] \]
\State \Return Standardized contraction component and its \texttt{computation\_id}.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Memory planning and execution}
\label{sec:memory_execution}

The scheduled operations define the lifetime of every intermediate tensor. A tensor is released after
its last use, and tensors with non-overlapping lifetimes share the same buffer, so that the peak
memory of a calculation is known from the plan before it runs. At run time (lower part of
Fig.~\ref{fig:symcorr_workflow}), \textsc{SymCorr} checks
this requirement against the memory of the requested device, a CPU or a single GPU, or selects one
automatically; if a GPU cannot hold the complete working set, the LapH-mode indices are tiled and the
data stay in host memory.
Independent source--sink time pairs of one gauge configuration are evaluated together with an
additional batch index whenever memory allows. Results are written incrementally to an HDF5 file, and
a work unit is marked complete only after its values have been written, so that an interrupted run
continues with the unfinished work units. Further runtime optimizations, such as prefetching
perambulator data and caching repeated kernels, do not change the plan or the numerical results.
