\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The strong coupling of Quantum ChromoDynamics (QCD) at low energies precludes the use of perturbative approaches, where hadrons emerge as the relevant effective degrees of freedom. Consequently, hadron physics investigations in the low energy regime rely on nonperturbative approaches such as those using the lattice QCD framework. Generation of lattice QCD ensembles through Monte-Carlo simulations and estimation of fermion matrix determinant as well as inversions of the fermion matrix consume substantial fraction of the computer time in lattice QCD calculation and have been extensively discussed and investigated since the inception of lattice QCD \cite{}. Physical observables in hadron-physics investigations are extracted from Euclidean correlation functions. In the early years of lattice QCD, these calculations primarily targeted ground-state hadron properties and were comparatively inexpensive. The discovery and study of numerous heavy and excited hadrons over the past several decades, however, necessitated increasingly sophisticated spectroscopy calculations, notably those employing Euclidean correlation matrices and variational analyses to determine the finite-volume spectrum and its properties. As the size and complexity of these correlation matrices increase, the associated operator construction, Wick contractions, and numerical evaluation become increasingly demanding, resulting in substantial rise in computational costs.

Modern day hadron spectroscopy proceeds through evaluation of two point Euclidean correlation matrices 
\beq 
C_{ij}(t-t_0)=\left\langle\mathcal O_i(t)\,\mathcal O_j^{\dagger}(t_0)\right\rangle
\eeq{2ptCfn}
for a basis of interpolators/operators $\{O_i\}$ that carry the quantum numbers of the desired hadronic state, where $t$ and $t_0$ refers to the sink and the source timeslices. In this context, the new challenges include a systematic construction of interpolating operators that enter the evaluation of correlation matrices, handling the allowed Wick contractions for every combination of interpolating operators, keeping track of the fermionic signs with permutations, flavor, spin, color and spatial indices faithfully, etc. The amount of bookkeeping therefore grows as one moves from meson operators to baryons, multi-hadron operators and nuclei. Additional conversions and reductions need to be made to express the correlation matrices in terms of the objects defining operator structures as well as the fermion matrix inverses. Note that different matrix elements carry similarities and share quark-propagation data, operator-dependent building blocks, and often portions of the resulting contraction expressions. This observation suggests opportunities to identify repeated (sub)objects IO and computations, reducing the computational redundancy. While a systematic road-map has already been laid out for such excited hadron spectroscopic investigations in the literature, performing them manually for every new channel being studied requires substantial implementation effort and can introduce bookkeeping errors.

There already exists tools to handle the operator construction and correlation matrix evaluation. Tools such as Multi-Hadron Interpolators (MHI)~\cite{PhysRevD.109.094516} and \texttt{OpTion}~\cite{Yan:2025op} provides a systematic construction of multi-hadron lattice operators projected to finite-volume irreps, total lab frame momentum, spin, and internal quantum numbers. The QCT package \cite{Djukanovic:2016spv} generates \texttt{C++} codes compatible with \texttt{QDP++} \cite{Edwards:2004sx} to perform Wick contractions for a given set of hadronic currents/operators expressed in terms of (anti)quark fields, without further optimization for computational efficiency. The Redstar framework ~\cite{10.1145/3592979.3593409} expresses each element in correlation matrices as a sum of graphs (Wick contractions) and utilizes uses graph-contraction algorithms to reduce redundant evaluations and to organize optimized correlator evaluations in a distillation-based calculational setup \cite{Peardon:2009gh} using tensor contractions for execution on CPUs and GPUs. Another recent algorithmic work explored determinant representations of Wick contractions and methods for identifying smaller sets of determinant terms that are sufficient for evaluating nuclear correlation functions~\cite{PhysRevD.110.114505}.

The emphasis of the \textsc{SymCorr} package is on having a complete automated transformation from a symbolic quark-level operator description to a numerical calculation within the distillation prescription~\cite{Peardon:2009gh} as a sequence of explicit compilation stages. Once the user provides the symbolic definition of a basis of operators in terms of quark and gauge fields, the operator representations, Wick contractions, automated translation to backend-independent physics objects, distillation lowering, execution planning, and numerical evaluation are organized as distinct layers of the software. This staged organization reduces manual intervention in correlator-specific algebra and implementation, while maintaining a clear separation between the physical definition of an operator and the details of its numerical evaluation. It also makes the intermediate algebraic transformations explicit and amenable to inspection and validation, while allowing numerical execution strategies to evolve and adapt independent of the underlying physics description of the interpolating operators.

\section{Overview and scope of \textsc{SymCorr}}
\label{sec:background}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\textsc{SymCorr} package facilitates the computation of Euclidean correlation matrices in the distillation framework, serving itself as a generalized automated platform in computation of arbitrary multi-hadron correlation matrices in finite-volume, with the assumption that the relevant lattice numerical data and the operator basis details are provided by the user. We begin this section by briefly discussing numerical objects relevant for the distillation framework for completeness. In the conventional non-distillation setup, the measurements involve computation of correlation matrices given in Eq. \ref{eq:2ptCfn} for a given operator basis and varying values of $t$ and $t_0$. These involve appropriate tensor index contractions between spatial-color-spin indices of the hadron interpolators with those in the quark line objects referred to as the quark propagators. 

The computational cost of such tensor index contractions spanning through the space-color-spin index values ($\mathcal{O}(10^6)$ for a $40^3$ spatial lattice) can become prohibitively expensive when then system being studied is large or complex such as multi-hadron operators, tetraquarks, pentaquarks, nuclei, etc. In the distillation framework, the combined space-color index is replaced by a single index, which could run over a appropriately chosen eigenmode subset of discretized gauge-covariant Laplacian $\{v^{(i)}(t)\}$. Such a replacement can be facilitated through quark smearing program defined in terms of $\{v^{(i)}(t)\}$ as 
\begin{align}
\tilde q(t)=\Box(t)q(t), &\qquad   
\tilde{\bar q}(t)=\bar q(t)\Box(t), \nonumber \\
\mbox{~~where ~~~} \Box(t)&=V(t)V^\dagger(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N_v}|v^{(i)}><v^{(i)}|(t),  \nonumber \\ 
\mbox{~and ~~~} V(t)&=\left(v^{(1)}(t),v^{(2)}(t),\ldots,v^{(N_v)}(t)\right).
\end{align}
The $\Box(t)$ operator acts as a low pass filter or a projection operator, with the lowest $N_v$ modes. Inserting such projection operators for the quark fields in the conventional correlations, followed regrouping the objects to form new objects such as 
\begin{align}
\tau_f(t,t_0) &= V^\dagger(t) M_f^{-1}(t,t_0) V(t_0) \\ 
\Phi_{\mathcal{O}}(t)&= V^\dagger(t)\,\mathcal{O}\,V(t).
\end{align}
referred to as the perambulators and elementals/kernels, which takes over the information from the quark propagator $M_f^{-1}(t,t_0)$ and hadronic operator $\Phi_A(t)$, respectively. The objects $\tau_f(t,t_0)$ and $\Phi_{\mathcal{O}}$ carry indices running of the $N_v\sim100-1000$ modes considered facilitating contractions of tensor indices with reduced computational cost. This novel procedure was not only found to reduce the computational cost requirement involved in tensor contractions, but also found to provide access to all-to-all quark propagators, to source momentum projection in hadron operator, multi-hadron operators, etc. The expensive quark-propagation information can be prepared independently of a particular spectroscopy operator basis, while changes to momenta, spin structures, or spatial constructions modify the operator-dependent factors. Within the distillation framework, the correlation matrix elements can be written in a compact form as 
\begin{equation}
C_{ij}(t,t_0)= \sum_{\alpha} c_{\alpha}\,\operatorname{Contr}_{\alpha}\left[\{\Phi\},\{\tau\}\right],
\label{eq:distillation_contraction_schematic}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ runs over the Wick-contractions ``$\operatorname{Contr}_{\alpha}\left[...\right]$'' and $c_{\alpha}$ carries the
associated numerical coefficient and the fermionic sign. 

The \textsc{SymCorr} package is designed to separate the physical definition and representation of correlation measurements from the details of their numerical evaluation. The user-provided interpolators determine the Wick contractions and their representation in the distillation space, as illustrated schematically in Eq.~\ref{eq:distillation_contraction_schematic}, while the subsequent numerical procedures, including tensor contractions, redundancy optimizations to avoid repetitive evaluations, memory (de)allocation, and data reuse, are handled by the \textsc{SymCorr} backend. The physics-level description of the measurements are preserved at every stage of the \textsc{SymCorr} flowchart to facilitate user-end as well as backend verifications and troubleshooting, while the numerical tensor objects and operations remain blind to the physics that is aimed at. The correlation matrix evaluation is treated within the \textsc{SymCorr} package as a single structured computational problem,  instead of compiling every matrix element as an independent numerical program. This helps in identifying similar algebraic expressions as well as redundant mathematical work across the full calculation and thus providing efficient choices for tensor contraction ordering and data reuse, enabling an optimized workflow. Following the optimization aprogram t the algebraic as well numerical level, \textsc{SymCorr} also makes educated decisions at the execution stage in regrouping calculations to utilize the provided the hardware resources to its maximum potential. All optimizations, at the algebraic, numerical, and execution stages, are handled by the \textsc{SymCorr} backend while preserving the underlying physics information. This shields non-expert users from the complexities of optimization, while still giving expert users full freedom to inspect the practical implementation and numerical performance.

We present the \textsc{SymCorr} workflow in Fig.~\ref{fig:symcorr_workflow}. The user provides the interpolator basis in symbolic form (Sec.~\ref{sec:symbolic}) together with a job specification that fixes the lattice dimensions, the number of distillation modes, the spin basis, the data locations, and the source and sink times. The calculation then proceeds in two stages. The compile stage uses only the symbolic operators and the compile settings. Linear combinations of operators are expanded into basis correlators, and all quark-line pairings (Wick contractions), their fermionic signs, and their coefficients are generated for every element of the correlation matrix (Sec.~\ref{sec:wick}). The resulting expressions are lowered to the distillation framework, where each Wick term becomes a tensor network of operator-dependent spatial kernels, propagation (perambulator) resources, and spin tensors (Sec.~\ref{sec:distillation_lowering}). All tensor networks of the requested correlation matrix are then treated as one global contraction problem: identical resources and identical contraction networks are shared across Wick terms and matrix elements, exactly equal terms are combined, a contraction order is chosen, shared intermediates are identified, and the operations are scheduled together with a plan for reusing memory buffers (Sec.~\ref{sec:execution}). The result is saved as a portable plan that depends neither on lattice data nor on hardware. In the run stage, the saved plan is first checked against the current operator definitions and job specification. \textsc{SymCorr} then chooses a CPU or GPU deployment and a memory-compatible batching of time slices for the available hardware, reads the Laplacian eigenvectors $\{v^{(i)}(t)\}$, the perambulators $\tau_f(t,t_0)$ and, for derivative or displaced operators, the gauge configuration in the form of the stout-smeared spatial links used to construct the eigenvectors, builds the required kernels and propagation tensors, and executes the frozen plan for every gauge configuration and time context. The correlation functions are written to an HDF5 file; a record of the run and a human-readable report can be produced on request. 

Now we present the scope of the \textsc{SymCorr} package. The present implementation can serve in optimized distillation-based correlation matrix evaluations and works on both CPU as well as GPU resources. The package assumes externally supplied operator basis, lattice QCD configuration, associated Laplacian eigenvectors, and the quark perambulators. Operator constructions such as those dealt by packages (MHI) and \texttt{OpTion} supplied n Ref. \cite{PhysRevD.109.094516,Yan:2025op}, generation of the lattice QCD configurations \cite{openQCD, MILC} and evaluation of Laplacian eigenvectors, and perambulators \cite{quada_laph} are outside \textsc{SymCorr}'s scope. The package can inspect the available resources such as CPUs, GPUs, memory, and can make educated decisions on efficient execution, however it does not support multi-GPU and MPI based multi-CPU evaluations, which is a straightforward extension. These are limited execution boundaries rather than restrictions on the symbolic definition of the correlator.

At the physics level, the \textsc{SymCorr} package has been validated for a variety of local as well as bilocal hadron operators, and their full distillation two point correlation matrix evaluations. The results has been cross-verified with independent Chroma based evaluations for rest as well as (multiple) moving frames, for single mesons, single baryons as well as bilocal two-hadron interpolators.  Correlation matrices with local four-quark operator and relavant bilocal two meson operators in the $T_{1g}$ have also been tested and cross-verified with an independent realization in the Croma software suite. Beyond this explicitly verified cases, the package has additional capabilities, that includes, but not limited to, multi-hadron correlation matrix evaluations, derivative-based single hadron correlator evaluations, correlation matrices involving disconnected quark loops, three point correlation matrices provided the generalized perambulators \cite{}.

\section{Symbolic Representation of Interpolating Operators}
\label{sec:symbolic}

The \textsc{SymCorr} package takes in the interpolator basis as a list of ordered algebraic expressions built out of objects representing (anti)quark fields, gauge fields, spin and color structures. At this stage of interpolator definition, the representation is independent of distillation tensors (perambulators and operator kernels/elementals), data layouts, contraction paths, and execution backends. 

Quark and antiquark fields of flavor f $\in\{u,d,c,s,b\}$ are represented schematically as $q_{\rm f}(x)_{a\alpha}\equiv\mbox{\texttt{f("x", "a", "alpha")}}$ ~and $\bar q_f(x)_{a\alpha}\equiv \mbox{\texttt{fbar("x", "a", "alpha")}}$, where $x$, $a$, and $\alpha$ denote position, color, and Dirac spin indices, respectively. The explicit flavor information and the quark/antiquark identification in the fermion object names aid in identifying flavor compatibility and in keeping track of fermionic permutation signatures in Wick contractions at the symbolic stage. The literal names chosen for dummy spin, color, and position indices are not part of the mathematical identity of any symbolic expression or the operator built out if it. The package assumes the following convention for the index labels. When a label is first introduced, its index type (spin, color or space) is fixed; attempting to reuse the same label for a different type will be rejected. Reusing the label within the same type defines the intended tensor connectivity of the operator.

Until the backend stage of numerical evaluation, no numerical conventions are assumed for the Dirac gamma structures. At the front end, the user may provide usual gamma matrices to represent the operator spin structures, examples of which will be given later in this section. On the color index, the package supports the Kronecker delta $\delta_{ab}$ for the meson-like objects and the antisymmetric Levi-Civita tensor $\epsilon_{abc}$ for the baryon-like operators. Spin and color indices follow the usual Einstein repeated index contraction convention within a symbolic expression. A single spin index occurrence remains open and can be used to represent the uncontracted Dirac spin index, such as in baryon operators. A single color index is allowed by the representation but will be reported as a warning as the resulting operator will be gauge dependent. Spin or color indices appearing more than twice are rejected.

Position indices identify the spatial locality associated with fermionic fields within an expression and are therefore not subject to the Einstein summation convention. Two or more fermionic fields carrying the same position label belong to the same locality group, whereas distinct labels can be used to represent multi-local or more general composite operators. This convention helps in assigning a definite three-momentum to each locality group as 
\begin{equation}
 x_r \longmapsto \mathbf p_r,
 \qquad
 \mathbf P = \sum_{r=1}^{n_l} \mathbf p_r ,
\label{eq:operator_momentum_groups}
\end{equation}
where $n_l$, $\mathbf p_r$ and $\mathbf P$ are the number of locality groups within, the three-momentum of locality group labelled $r$ and the total three-momentum carried by the full symbolic expression. In this sense, the position labels represent the momentum orientations within the symbolic expression. Spatial transformations such as pure translations, gauge-covariant parallel transport, covariant derivatives, including the directional information on the fermionic fields are also supported by dedicated objects: \texttt{shift}, \texttt{transport}, and \texttt{derivative}. 

Large symbolic expressions that are representative of hadronic operators can thus be constructed as products of elementary fermion field objects, with the factor ordering retained explicitly to ensure faithful evaluation permutation signs during Wick contractions. Momentum information and linear combinations of such expressions can then be assigned to build composite interpolating operators, with further freedom to provide additional metadata. The user-provided operator expressions are subjected to a deterministic index relabeling while preserving the flavor, fermionic and factor ordering, contracted indices and the associated spatial and momentum assignments. This backend relabeling procedure aids in identifying same and similar operator structures. The sink and source operator bases may be provided as separate ordered objects by the user. When an explicit source basis is not supplied, the source operator basis is constructed as the Hermitian conjugate of the sink operator basis. 

Now we provide various examples of input operator structures from which more complicated operators can be further built. A generic local meson-like operator is given by a quark bilinear $\mathcal O_M(x)=\bar q_{f_1}(x)_{a\alpha}\Gamma_{\alpha\beta} q_{f_2}(x)_{a\beta}$. For the isovector pseudoscalar pion operator, this is given by $\mathcal O_{\pi}(x) = \bar d(x)\,\gamma_5\,u(x)$, where the spin-color indices are implicit. The input expression for this operator projected to zero momentum at the sink is given by 
\begin{verbatim}
pionsnk = Operator( dbar("x", "a", "alpha")
                *gamma5("alpha", "beta")
                *u("x", "a", "beta"),
                 momentum=(0, 0, 0),).
\end{verbatim}
The corresponding source operator can be built from the operator definition at the sink as \texttt{pionsrc = pionsnk.dagger()}.

The conventionally used local baryon-like operator structure is given by diquark-passenger quark form given by $\mathcal O_B(x)_\delta=\epsilon_{abc}[q_1(x)_{a\alpha}\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}q_2(x)_{b\beta}]q_3(x)_{c\delta}$, where $\Gamma$ carries the spin structure of the diquark, whereas the $\delta$ is the Dirac spin index of the passenger quark $q_3$ as well as the baryon. Simplest such quark trilinear operator for a proton can be build by the assignments $q_1\rightarrow u$, $q_3\rightarrow u$, $q_2\rightarrow d$ and $\Gamma\rightarrow C\gamma_5$. The symbolic representation of such a proton operator is given by 
\begin{verbatim}
proton = Operator( epsilon("a", "b", "c")
                  *cgamma5("alpha", "beta")
                  *u("x", "a", "alpha")
                  *d("x", "b", "beta")
                  *u("x", "c", "delta"),
                   momentum=(0, 0, 0),).
\end{verbatim}
\textsc{SymCorr} also comes with a convenience constructor for such diquark-passenger quark form of baryon-like operators, using which a proton operator can be built as 
\begin{verbatim}
proton = build_baryon_operator( 
         ["u", "d", "u"], "Cg5",
         momentum=(0, 0, 0),)
\end{verbatim}
More complex local hadron operators can further be constructed from these elementary objects and constructions. A local scalar diquark-antidiquark structure representing a compact tetraquark operator can then be represented as
\begin{verbatim}
dqadq_local = Operator(  epsilon("a", "b", "e") 
                       * u("x", "a", "alpha") 
                       * cgamma5("alpha", "beta") 
                       * d("x", "b", "beta")
                       * epsilon("c", "d", "e")
                       * ubar("x", "c", "gamma") 
                       * cgamma5("gamma", "delta") 
                       * dbar("x", "d", "delta"),
                       momentum=(0, 0, 0),).
\end{verbatim}
Another example of a simple tetraquark operator is that of a local two-meson structure, which can be represented as 
\begin{verbatim}
two_meson_local = Operator(  dbar("x", "a", "alpha")
                           * gamma5("alpha", "beta")
                           * u("x", "a", "beta")
                           * ubar("x", "b", "gamma")
                           * gamma5("gamma", "delta")
                           * d("x", "b", "delta"),
                           momentum=(0, 0, 0),).
\end{verbatim}
Note that in the above construction there are two color singlet quark bilinears forming two independent mesons both featuring in the same locality group leading to a single momentum assignment for the entire structure. 

Spatially extended operators can be built by augmenting relevant constituent elementary field as argument to the operator objects: \texttt{shift}, \texttt{transport}, and \texttt{derivative}. Below are three examples of simple meson operators (projected to zero momentum) with nontrivial spatial structures. First one is $\mathcal{O}_{M_1}=\bar d(x)\gamma_5\nabla_{x}(u(x))$ that features a symmetric derivative in the $x$ direction and the corresponding symbolic expression is given by 
\begin{verbatim}
mesondx = Operator(  dbar("x", "a", "al")
                   * gamma5("al", "be")
                   * derivative(u("x", "a", "be"),"+x",),
                   momentum=(0, 0, 0),). 
\end{verbatim}

Composite operators such as bilocal or multilocal operators could be built as products of simpler structures. Let $H_1(\mathbf p_1)$ and $H_2(\mathbf p_2)$ be independently defined operators with distinct momentum assignments, their product is given by $\mathcal O_{12} = H_1(\mathbf p_1)\,H_2(\mathbf p_2)$, where $\mathbf P=\mathbf p_1+\mathbf p_2$ is the total three-momentum of the operator $\mathcal O_{12}$. In the symbolic form, individual expressions for mesons can be concatenated to form the composite operator expression either explicitly or implicitly. In the latter strategy, one defines individual mesons as  
\begin{verbatim}
meson1 = Operator(  dbar("x", "a", "alpha")
                  * gamma5("alpha", "beta")
                  * u("x", "a", "beta"),
                  momentum=(1, 0, 0),)
\end{verbatim}
and 
\begin{verbatim}
meson2 = Operator(  ubar("y", "b", "gamma")
                  * gamma5("gamma", "delta")
                  * d("y", "b", "delta"),
                  momentum=(-1, 0, 0),).
\end{verbatim}
Following these definitions, the bi-local two-meson operator, where the individual mesons are projected to definite momentum can be represented as 
\begin{verbatim}
two_meson_bilocal = meson1 * meson2.
\end{verbatim}
Alternatively, one can define the same explicitly as 
\begin{verbatim}
two_meson_bilocal = Operator(  dbar("x", "a", "alpha")
                             * gamma5("alpha", "beta")
                             * u("x", "a", "beta")
                             * ubar("y", "b", "gamma")
                             * gamma5("gamma", "delta")
                             * d("y", "b", "delta"),
                             momenta={"x": (1, 0, 0),
                                    "y": (-1, 0, 0),},),
\end{verbatim}
where the individual mesons fall into separate locality groups represented by $x$ and $y$, which have momentum assignments $\mathbf p_x=(1, 0, 0)$ and $\mathbf p_y=(-1, 0, 0)$, respectively.

Another important feature supported by the package is the construction of complete composite operators, which are typically linear combinations various expressions $\mathcal O =\sum_i c_i\,\mathcal O_i$. Addition, subtraction, scalar multiplication, and products of simpler expressions are also accepted as user-defined operator expressions, facilitating such composite operator expression representations. Hermitian conjugation acts on the full symbolic construction, combined with a reversed sign for all the associated momenta. Thus, upon Hermitian conjugation of $\mathcal O$, the fermionic field order in each $\mathcal O_i$ is reversed and each field object in $\mathcal O_i$ is conjugated according to its symbolic rule. Whereas for a linear combination, the coefficients $c_i$ are complex conjugated and each basis operator $\mathcal O_i$ is Hermitian conjugated following the rule for product operator. These operations allow a source operator to be derived from a sink definition without manually reconstructing its field ordering, spin structure, or momentum orientation.

\section{Quark propagation and Wick contraction}
\label{sec:wick}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Following the user-input of interpolator basis, symbolic representation of the correlation matrix to be evaluated is instantiated, then all allowed quark propagations are identified, thereafter the correlation matrix is reformed in terms of quark lines or quark propagators. To this end, the operator linear combinations are expanded to build a new correlation matrix for an expanded definite basis of single term operator expressions. This expanded basis aids in identifying redundancies and optimizing evaluation cost and the final correlation matrix in the original basis will be reconstructed at a later stage after symbolic and numerical optimization as well as evaluation stages. The next stage in \texttt{SymCorr} is in determining all allowed combinatorial pairing of quark-antiquark pairs for every quark flavor appearing in each of the correlation matrix element in the larger definite basis. For each quark flavor, the number of quark fields should be same as the number of antiquark fields in any given correlation matrix element considered. If this condition fails for any flavor, no contraction term would be evaluated for that matrix element. 

Once the quark-antiquark field balance is ensured for each flavor, every bijection between the quark and antiquark fields of the each flavor is identified. $n_{\rm f}$ quark fields of a given flavor leads to $n_{\rm f}!$ possible pairings. Since the choices for different flavors are independent, the complete set of flavor-compatible pairings is given by $N_{\rm pair}=\prod_{\rm f} n_{\rm f}!$. Note that all quark-antiquark fields bijections generated are based on the flavor information explicitly specified in the elementary quark field objects that compose the hadronic operator. For example, $u$ and $d$ remain distinct quark flavors at this stage, even if isospin is assumed to be a good symmetry leading to the use of same light quark propagator/perambulator for either flavors at a later stage of the evaluation. Pairing of quark and antiquark fields of the same flavor when both occur on the same side of a two-point function or within the same composite insertion is also considered as a valid bijection. Retaining all allowed possibilities is necessary to realize the correlations, which may include closed quark loops or disconnected or mixed contraction topologies, to project the desired quantum numbers correctly. All admissible field pairings determined at this stage are retained explicitly and are passed on to the next stage discussed below. 

Complete tensor connection within each admissible field pairing leads to a diagram, which is referred to as a Wick contraction diagram and is associated with a unique relative sign. For a given Wick contraction, this sign is fixed by the number parity of the fermionic field reordering required to arrive at the corresponding pairing expression from the original symbolic correlation matrix element. Other tensor objects such as spin matrices, color structures and spatial transformations within the correlation matrix element remain part of the correlator expression, and do not contribute to the fermion permutation signature. Consider a correlation matrix element with $N$ quark fields and thus $N$ antiquark fields, with the original user-defined fermionic field order $F$. For each given Wick contraction associated with the correlation mattrix element, \texttt{SymCorr} builds the fermionic field ordering in which each quark is placed immediately left to its paired antiquark, for example 
\begin{equation}
F_{\mathcal P} =(q_{i_1},\bar q_{j_1},\ldots, q_{i_N},\bar q_{j_N}).
\end{equation}
The fermion permutation sign is given by $s_{\mathcal P}=(-1)^{I(\mathcal P)}$, the number parity of fermion field permutations that maps the original field ordering $F$ to the paired ordering $F_{\mathcal P}$. 

We demonstrate this exercise with a simple example of correlation function with four fermion fields. Suppose the original fermionic order is $F=(q_1,\,q_2,\,\bar q_1,\,\bar q_2)$. For the pairing $(q_1,\bar q_1), (q_2,\bar q_2)$, the paired sequence corresponds to $F_{\mathcal P} =(q_{1},\bar q_{1},q_{2},\bar q_{2})$. Mapping $F$ to $F_{\mathcal P}$ involves one permutation and hence has sign -1. The only other admissible pairing $(q_1,\bar q_2), (q_2,\bar q_1)$, the pairing sequence $F_{\mathcal P} =(q_{1},\bar q_{2},q_{2},\bar q_{1})$. In this case, mapping $F$ to $F_{\mathcal P}$ requires two fermion field permutation and hence has sign +1. 

Each Wick contraction, within a correlation matrix element, comes also with an algebraic coefficient $c_{\mathcal P}$, which might be originating from the coefficients in the operator linear combinations within in the user-defined basis. These factors are appropriately combined into the coefficient multiplying the logical correlator $c_{\mathcal P}^{\rm full}=s_{\mathcal P}\,c_{\mathcal P}$. Note that the sign and the algebraic coefficients are kept as separate pieces of the Wick contraction. This aids in making the origin of permutation sign tracable and thus 
inspectable. 

\subsection{Canonicalization and symbolic simplification}

Canonicalization in SymCorr is used to identify symbolic descriptions that are
mathematically equivalent under rules that are explicit in the implementation.
It is not used to guess additional field-theory identities. In particular, the
frontend retains the expression exactly as supplied by the user, while a
separate structural identity removes dependence on arbitrary dummy-index names.
Contracted (dummy) spin, color, and position labels are renamed
deterministically, and the momentum map is transformed through the same
position-label mapping. Open indices keep their names, so that, for example, an
operator and its transpose in two open spin indices remain distinct. Ordered
factor structure, flavor, transformations, and momentum values remain part of
the operator identity, whereas user metadata is treated as annotation rather
than symbolic physics.

A second canonicalization step occurs at the compiler boundary before Wick
contraction. Field-operation chains are normalized without mutating the
frontend operator. For pure coordinate shifts and gauge-covariant parallel
transport, adjacent inverse lattice hops cancel. For example,

\begin{equation}
(+x,-x,+y)\longrightarrow(+y).
\label{eq:path_canonicalization}
\end{equation}

Nested field operations are then fused into a compiler-internal composite
operation that preserves their order. Other primitive operations are retained
unchanged by this pass, and the operator momentum assignments and metadata are
copied to the normalized compiler-side representation.

This normalization is deliberately performed before linear-combination terms
are merged. Two frontend operators can therefore remain visibly different as
user objects yet become the same compiler basis operator when, for example,
their transport paths differ only by adjacent inverse hops. Once normalized,
weights multiplying identical ordered basis-operator tuples are added, and a
basis term is removed only when its weight cancels exactly; no numerical
tolerance is applied. For a two-point correlation matrix, the corresponding
simplification pass performs the same coefficient aggregation separately within
each matrix element for identical normalized sink--source basis pairs.

The same normalized operator identity is also used at the Wick-cache boundary.
The cache key is the complete ordered tuple of basis-operator insertions rather
than an unordered set. Consequently, repeated occurrences of the same
normalized symbolic correlator can reuse an already generated Wick expansion,
while a physically different operator ordering remains a distinct contraction
problem. This reuse avoids repeated symbolic work but does not alter the set of
Wick pairings or their fermionic signs.

The scope of these transformations is intentionally restricted. The pre-Wick
normalizer does not commute or reorder fermion factors, replace spin or color
structures using additional identities, infer cyclic trace equivalences, or
simplify expressions using numerical tensor values. Such transformations could
change the field ordering or require assumptions that belong to later compiler
representations. At this stage SymCorr therefore applies only the
canonicalization rules for which equivalence is explicit in the symbolic
algebra.

There is a separate simplification mechanism later in the compilation pipeline,
after the Wick terms have been lowered to numerical contraction graphs. At that
stage exact global common-subexpression analysis can canonicalize reusable
tensor-network components and combine exactly equal complete Wick terms within
a correlator. Like the pre-Wick weights, the later complete-term aggregation removes only
exactly cancelling coefficients, and it does so only for scalar terms. Keeping these two stages distinct avoids
conflating symbolic algebra with numerical contraction reuse; the latter is
discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:execution}.

\subsection{Output of the contraction stage}

For each normalized basis correlator, the Wick engine returns an ordered
collection of symbolic Wick terms. A convenient schematic description of one
such term is

\begin{equation}
W_\alpha
=
\left(
 s_\alpha,
 c_\alpha,
 \mathcal P_\alpha,
 \mathcal E_\alpha,
 \mathcal C_\alpha
\right),
\label{eq:wick_term_contents}
\end{equation}

where \(s_\alpha\) is the fermionic sign, \(c_\alpha\) is the algebraic
coefficient, \(\mathcal P_\alpha\) is the explicit set of paired quark and
antiquark fields, \(\mathcal E_\alpha\) is the contracted symbolic expression,
and \(\mathcal C_\alpha\) records the locality-graph components inferred from
the pairing. These quantities remain symbolic: no perambulator, distillation
kernel, tensor shape, external file, memory buffer, or execution device has yet
been introduced.

The pairing \(\mathcal P_\alpha\) is retained directly rather than being
recoverable only from a printed algebraic expression. Each entry identifies the
quark endpoint and the antiquark endpoint of one logical propagation line,
including their flavor and symbolic spin, color, and position labels. This
explicit pairing is the authoritative input used by the next compiler boundary
to construct logical quark lines. It also retains the information needed to
associate field-level derivatives, transports, shifts, and spatial
representations with the correct endpoints.

The symbolic expression \(\mathcal E_\alpha\) retains the non-fermionic tensor
content inherited from the original operators and represents contracted quark
lines symbolically. In particular, spin matrices, color tensors, gauge-link
objects, and their index connectivity remain visible rather than being replaced
by numerical arrays at the Wick stage. The later physics-lowering pass reads
these symbolic tensors and the explicit pairing together; it does not infer the
quark-line topology from numerical tensor shapes.

In addition to the algebraic term, SymCorr computes a locality graph from the
pairing. Position labels form the graph vertices and a contracted quark line
connects the position labels of its two endpoints. Each resulting graph
component records both the locality labels that it contains and the operator
insertions touched by those labels. The present Wick-level topology label is a
coarse diagnostic: a term is classified as \emph{disconnected} when all
components are local to individual operator insertions, \emph{connected} when
there are spanning components and no purely local component, and \emph{mixed}
when spanning and local components occur together. This terminology does not
assert that the complete numerical tensor network consists of one connected
component.

For composite operators, the same component records provide an additional
structural diagnostic. The current \texttt{factorized} property is true when no
locality component spans more than two operator insertions; a component touching
more than two insertions marks a multi-operator exchange structure. This
information is useful for inspecting the generated Wick terms, but it is kept
separate from later numerical common-subexpression analysis and contraction
planning.

When a basis correlator originates from a linear combination, its previously
computed complex basis weight is applied to the Wick-term coefficient while the
cached sign, pairing, expression, and component structure remain unchanged. The
next lowering step then combines sign and coefficient and translates each Wick
term into a backend-independent logical correlator. In the current PhysicsIR,
the resulting records contain logical quark lines, spin tensors, color tensors,
gauge tensors, field-operation information, and momentum assignments. The
Wick-level locality-component and topology diagnostics are not part of this
PhysicsIR data contract.

This boundary is important because it marks the end of the purely symbolic Wick
problem. By the time a term leaves this stage, SymCorr has fixed which fermion
fields contract, the associated Grassmann sign, the algebraic weight, and the
symbolic operator structures that accompany those propagation lines. The next
stage may reorganize this information into backend-independent physics records,
but it is not permitted to invent a new pairing or reinterpret the fermionic
sign.

\subsection{Distillation primitives}

The input to distillation lowering is the validated PhysicsIR described at the
end of Sec.~\ref{sec:wick}. At this boundary the fermionic pairing and
coefficient of each Wick contribution are already fixed. The purpose of the
distillation layer is therefore not to redo the field-theory algebra, but to
translate the physical records into a small set of objects that describe the
spatial/color structures and propagation resources required by a distillation
calculation.

The first primitive is a \emph{projected field endpoint}. Every endpoint in
PhysicsIR carries an explicit spatial representation, currently either LapH or
POINT, together with any field-local derivative, transport, or shift operations.
For a LapH endpoint, the compiler realizes the contribution according to the
orientation of the fermion field,

\begin{equation}
q_{\mathrm{LAPH}} \longmapsto V,
\qquad
\bar q_{\mathrm{LAPH}} \longmapsto V^\dagger,
\label{eq:laph_endpoint_realization}
\end{equation}

whereas an explicitly POINT endpoint remains a distinct point contribution.
This translation records which spatial object is required; it does not load an
eigenvector tensor or construct a numerical array. The operations attached to
the original endpoint remain associated with the realized field so that their
placement is not reconstructed later from tensor shape or ordering.

Before these projected fields are assembled, SymCorr partitions each logical
correlator into spatial--color components. The partition is determined from the
incidence of field endpoints, position labels, color labels, explicit color
tensors, and gauge-link tensors. Each connected component containing at least
one field endpoint becomes an operator vertex. Spin tensors do not determine
this partition. Consequently, the decomposition is independent of whether the
original symbolic object was described as a meson, baryon, tetraquark, or
multi-hadron operator; it follows only from the explicit spatial and color
connectivity already present in PhysicsIR.

A realized spatial--color component is represented by a \texttt{KernelRecipe}.
Conceptually, this recipe specifies the operator-dependent projected object that
will ultimately be built from the required eigenvector or point contributions,
field-local spatial transformations, color tensors, gauge tensors, position
variables, and momentum projections. For LapH fields its external spatial legs
are LapH-mode indices; POINT fields remain distinguishable and retain a
color-like external spatial leg. The recipe is therefore a compiler description
of the object to be materialized rather than the materialized tensor itself.

Spin structure is kept as a separate primitive. Gamma matrices and other spin
tensors have already been converted into explicit \texttt{SpinTensorRecord}
objects in PhysicsIR, and they are deliberately excluded from the identity of a
spatial/color \texttt{KernelRecipe}. This separation mirrors the distillation
factorization discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:background}: the reusable spatial
object and the spin structure that contracts with it need not be treated as one
opaque tensor at the compiler boundary. It also allows two contributions with
the same spatial/color construction but different spin structure to share the
same spatial resource where the later contraction graph permits it.

The remaining primitive class describes quark propagation between these
operator-dependent objects. From the exact logical quark lines, later stages
reconstruct open fermion paths and closed cycles and assign corresponding
propagation requirements. For an open path this requirement is represented by a
\texttt{PropagationRecipe}; with no inserted current it denotes an ordinary
perambulator requirement, while supported explicit POINT-current insertions can
produce a generalized propagation requirement. The detailed construction and
current implementation boundary of these resources are discussed below in the
subsection on propagation resources.

These objects should be distinguished from concrete numerical data. At this
stage a kernel recipe is not yet an evaluated elemental, and a propagation
recipe is not an in-memory perambulator. No filename, HDF5 group, source time,
tensor shape, physical buffer, CPU/GPU placement, or contraction schedule is
chosen by distillation realization. Those choices belong to later graph,
resource-binding, materialization, and execution stages. The output of this
layer is therefore a more concrete statement of the required distillation
calculation while remaining independent of a particular data layout or
machine.

\subsection{Operator-dependent structures}

The operator-dependent part of the distillation calculation is constructed from
the spatial--color components identified above rather than from a fixed library
of meson, baryon, or tetraquark tensor shapes. For one component \(A\), let
\(\mathbf x_r\) denote its position variables, \(\mathbf p_r\) the associated
momenta, and \(k_i\) the external LapH-mode indices carried by its projected
fields. A useful schematic form of the corresponding spatial kernel is

\begin{align}
\mathcal K_A(\{k_i\};t)
&=
\sum_{\{\mathbf x_r\},\{a_i\}}
\exp\!\left(
 i\sum_r \mathbf p_r\!\cdot\!\mathbf x_r
\right)
\mathcal C_A(\{a_i\})\nonumber\\
&\qquad\times\mathcal U_A(\{\mathbf x_r\},\{a_i\})
\prod_i
\mathcal V_i(\mathbf x_{r(i)},a_i,k_i;t),
\label{eq:generic_operator_spatial_kernel}
\end{align}

where \(\mathcal C_A\) denotes the explicit color-tensor structure,
\(\mathcal U_A\) denotes any explicit gauge-link structure represented in the
component, and \(\mathcal V_i\) denotes the appropriately oriented LapH
factor, including the field-local spatial operations attached to that endpoint.
For a quark endpoint this factor is built from \(V\), while for an antiquark
endpoint it is built from the conjugate/projected orientation corresponding to
\(V^\dagger\). Equation~\eqref{eq:generic_operator_spatial_kernel} is schematic:
its purpose is to display the information retained by the compiler rather than
to impose one fixed tensor rank or one fixed pattern of spatial indices.

For example, a local bilinear whose two LapH fields are contracted to a color
singlet gives a two-mode spatial kernel of the schematic form

\begin{equation}
K_{kl}^{(\mathbf p)}(t)
=
\sum_{\mathbf x,a}
 e^{+i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}
 \left[\mathcal D_{\bar q}V(t)\right]^*_{\mathbf x a k}
 \left[\mathcal D_q V(t)\right]_{\mathbf x a l},
\label{eq:bilinear_spatial_kernel}
\end{equation}

where \(\mathcal D_q\) and \(\mathcal D_{\bar q}\) stand for the spatial
operations attached to the two field endpoints. The spin matrix is not folded
into this kernel. In the simplest separable case the familiar elemental-like
object is obtained only after the independent spin structure is attached, for
example through a factor \(\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}K_{kl}\). This is the concrete
reason that SymCorr does not use the full spin--spatial elemental as its
reusable spatial resource identity.

The same construction applies without changing the lowering algorithm when the
component has higher rank. For a local three-quark color-singlet structure, the
spatial part has the schematic form

\begin{align}
B_{k_1k_2k_3}^{(\mathbf p)}(t)
&=
\sum_{\mathbf x,a,b,c}
 e^{+i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}
 \epsilon_{abc}
 \left[\mathcal D_1 V(t)\right]_{\mathbf x a k_1}\nonumber\\
&\qquad\times
 \left[\mathcal D_2 V(t)\right]_{\mathbf x b k_2}
 \left[\mathcal D_3 V(t)\right]_{\mathbf x c k_3}.
\label{eq:three_quark_spatial_kernel}
\end{align}

The diquark spin matrix and any remaining open spin index are represented
separately. Thus the difference between the bilinear and three-quark examples
appears through the number of projected fields and their explicit color
connectivity, not through a hadron-specific branch in the distillation
compiler.

Momentum is part of the spatial resource itself. SymCorr retains one momentum
assignment for each position group in a component and uses the phase convention
\(e^{+i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}\); Hermitian conjugation has already reversed
the corresponding source momentum where required. For a component containing
several independent position labels, the spatial object contains the product of
those phase factors, equivalently the exponential shown in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:generic_operator_spatial_kernel}. This keeps constituent momenta
visible for bilocal and multi-local constructions rather than replacing them by
only the total momentum.

The compiler assigns each \texttt{KernelRecipe} a deterministic spatial
resource identity. That identity is invariant under dummy position/color
renaming but includes the projected-field orientations, field-local operations,
color tensors, explicit gauge connectivity, and momentum assignments. Spin and
quark flavor are deliberately excluded from this spatial identity. Spin appears
as separate primitive tensor nodes in the subsequent contraction graph, while
the LapH eigenvector subspace itself is a gauge-field spatial object rather
than a flavor-dependent quark propagator. Equal spatial resource identities
therefore expose possible reuse without yet merging the distinct occurrences
that appear in different Wick terms or matrix elements; that global reuse is a
later optimization step.

For the currently executable exact-distillation path, the compatibility bridge
turns a spatial recipe into primitive operations for the required eigenvector
factors, momentum phases, and the supported \(\delta\) and \(\epsilon\) color
tensors. Derivative information remains attached to the field and can be
realized by the numerical tensor provider from derivative eigenvector data or,
when configured, from the underlying eigenvectors and gauge links. The recipe
layer is broader than this executable subset: explicit \texttt{GaugeLink}
tensor nodes can be represented in the spatial/color component, but the current
numerical kernel ISA rejects such standalone gauge-tensor nodes rather than
silently approximating them. This distinction is retained here so that symbolic
representability is not presented as equivalent to validated numerical
execution.

The result of this lowering is therefore an operator-dependent spatial resource
with explicit LapH ports, together with separate spin tensors whose indices are
connected only when the primitive contraction graph is built. This is the form
in which SymCorr carries the operator structure forward to the propagation and
contraction stages: sufficiently concrete to determine the required numerical
tensors, but still independent of the contraction order, memory layout, or
execution device.

\subsection{Propagation resources}

After the operator-dependent kernels have been identified, the Wick quark lines
must be mapped to the propagation tensors that connect their spatial and spin
ports. SymCorr performs this step from the exact fermion topology rather than
from operator names or tensor shapes. Each open quark path is decomposed into
its ordered Wick-propagator segments and any operator-local transitions that
join successive segments. The result is a logical propagation requirement; the
compiler still does not load or construct a numerical propagator.

For an ordinary path containing one Wick segment and no absorbed insertion, the
required object is the usual distillation perambulator introduced in
Sec.~\ref{sec:background}. The corresponding \texttt{PropagationRecipe}
records the exact source and sink spatial kernels, the particular open port on
each kernel, the flavor carried by the Wick segment, and the spatial
representation of the two endpoints. Schematically, its numerical realization
has the canonical index structure

\begin{equation}
\tau_f(t_{\mathrm{snk}},t_{\mathrm{src}})
\equiv
\tau_{f;\,k\alpha,\,l\beta}
(t_{\mathrm{snk}},t_{\mathrm{src}}),
\label{eq:propagation_resource_layout}
\end{equation}

where \((k,\alpha)\) are the sink LapH-mode and spin indices and
\((l,\beta)\) are the source indices. The orientation is inherited from the
Wick pairing and is therefore fixed before any external storage convention is
considered.

Open paths can also contain explicit local current insertions. In the current
compiler, a current is absorbed into a generalized propagation resource only
when its quark and antiquark endpoints are explicitly represented as POINT
fields belonging to one spatial--color component. For one insertion, the
logical structure is schematically

\begin{equation}
\mathcal T_{f_0\to f_1}^{J}
(t_2,t_1,t_0),
\label{eq:generalized_propagation_schematic}
\end{equation}

where the propagation segment before the current carries flavor \(f_0\), the
segment after it carries flavor \(f_1\), and \(J\) denotes the specified local
current structure. SymCorr does not assume that the two flavors are equal. A
flavor-changing current is represented as a change between adjacent propagation
segments rather than as a flavor change inside one Wick segment.

The generalized recipe retains substantially more information than only the
name of the current. For every absorbed insertion it stores the spatial kernel,
the exact incoming and outgoing kernel ports, the incoming and outgoing
flavors, the endpoint spin labels, and the independent spin tensors connecting
those labels. Several POINT bilinear insertions can be retained in path order by
the compiler representation. The deterministic propagation-resource identity
therefore depends on whether the object is ordinary or generalized, the source
and sink representations, the ordered sequence of segment flavors, and the
canonical spatial and spin content of the insertions. It deliberately does not
depend on a filename, HDF5 dataset name, source time, device, or execution
strategy.

This logical identity is separated from the occurrence at which the resource is
used. During resource binding, each compiler propagation resource is mapped to
one exact external tensor source. The occurrence placement then supplies the
frontend source, insertion, and sink operator slots, and the run-time
operator-time context converts those slots into concrete Euclidean times. Thus
two uses of the same mathematical propagation resource can request different
time slices without becoming different compiler resources. Conversely, the
binding layer does not use a fallback such as ``look up a tensor by flavor'';
ordinary and generalized resources are bound by their exact resource identity.

External storage conventions are normalized only when a concrete propagation
tensor is materialized. The canonical tensor expected by the contraction graph
is ordered as sink mode/spin followed by source mode/spin. For a fixed-source
file containing
\(P_{\mathrm{file}}(t,t_0)\), a direct request with
\(t_{\mathrm{src}}=t_0\) uses the stored orientation after the configured axis
conversion. If instead \(t_{\mathrm{snk}}=t_0\), SymCorr reconstructs the
reverse propagation using \(\gamma_5\)-Hermiticity,

\begin{equation}
P(t_0,t)
=
\gamma_5\,
P_{\mathrm{file}}(t,t_0)^\dagger
\,\gamma_5 .
\label{eq:fixed_source_reverse_propagation}
\end{equation}

The Hermitian adjoint exchanges both mode and spin source/sink spaces; a simple
complex conjugation is not equivalent. If neither requested endpoint is the
fixed source, that tensor cannot be obtained from the file and materialization
fails rather than inventing missing propagation data.

The materialization adapter also owns external source-field and spin-basis
conventions. In the validated QUDA-LapH input path, for example, a stored
\texttt{qdagger} source convention is converted to SymCorr's canonical
\(\langle q\bar q\rangle\) convention using the configured spin basis before
the tensor reaches the generic numerical executor. Likewise, a reverse
fixed-source transformation is applied in the adapter exactly once. The
contraction executor therefore consumes the propagation tensor in the
orientation requested by the compiler and does not apply an additional
post-hoc conjugation or \(\gamma_5\) correction.

Closed fermion cycles use a related but distinct lowering. For an exact LapH
closed cycle, SymCorr does not request a separately stored disconnected-loop
tensor. Instead, every Wick segment is represented by an ordinary perambulator
and the successive operator-local spatial and spin tensors close the cyclic
contraction. This route currently assumes ordinary all-time distillation
perambulators for the required segments. A genuinely POINT disconnected loop
cannot be reconstructed from those perambulators because they do not contain
the full point-space all-to-all propagator; such a loop therefore requires
additional stochastic, all-to-all, or precomputed-loop information and is
rejected by the present exact-distillation lowering.

The compiler and resource-binding layers can consequently represent ordinary
perambulators, ordered generalized POINT-current resources, and exact-LapH
closed-cycle propagation structure within one topology-driven framework. Their
validation status is nevertheless different. The ordinary perambulator path is
part of the external-data validation described in
Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}, whereas generalized propagation inputs and the broader
closed-loop pathways should be regarded as implemented compiler/runtime
capabilities unless a separate external numerical validation is stated later in
this paper.

\subsection{Construction of contraction tasks}

Once the operator-dependent kernels, propagation requirements, and spin tensors
have been identified, SymCorr converts each complete PhysicsIR Wick contribution
into a primitive tensor-contraction graph. This graph is the first compiler
representation in which the numerical contraction problem is explicit. It
contains the tensors that must participate in the Wick term and the indices
that connect them, but it still contains no decision about the order in which
those tensors will be contracted.

For a Wick term \(\alpha\), let \(T_{\alpha n}\) denote its primitive tensor
occurrences and let \(I_{\alpha n}\) denote the ordered set of contraction
indices attached to tensor \(n\). The graph represents the contribution
schematically as

\begin{equation}
C_\alpha
=
c_\alpha
\sum_{\{i\}_{\mathrm{internal}}}
\prod_n
T_{\alpha n}(I_{\alpha n}),
\label{eq:primitive_contraction_task}
\end{equation}

where \(c_\alpha\) is the coefficient already fixed in PhysicsIR. Indices that
occur on two primitive tensor legs are summed internal indices. An index that
occurs on only one leg remains a free output index, so the same graph
representation can describe both scalar correlator terms and terms with an
explicit open index. The graph validator rejects an index attached to more than
two primitive legs rather than assigning an ambiguous multiway contraction
meaning.

The current primitive graph has only three tensor kinds: operator-dependent
spatial kernels, propagation tensors, and spin tensors. A kernel node exposes
one port for every surviving external spatial leg of its
\texttt{KernelRecipe}. For a LapH kernel these ports become LapH-mode
contraction indices. A spin node exposes its left and right spin indices. A
propagation node connects the corresponding kernel and spin spaces using the
canonical leg order

\begin{equation}
(\text{sink mode},\text{sink spin},
 \text{source mode},\text{source spin}).
\label{eq:primitive_propagation_ports}
\end{equation}

The graph therefore records tensor connectivity explicitly rather than relying
on a later numerical backend to infer contractions from array shape or node
ordering.

Fermion topology determines which propagation nodes are inserted. Along an open
fermion path, POINT bilinear transitions that satisfy the generalized-resource
requirements described above are absorbed into the corresponding propagation
recipe. Their spatial kernel and spin tensors are then omitted as separate graph
nodes so that the current is not counted twice. A non-absorbed transition must
currently have LapH endpoints and remains explicit through its kernel and spin
tensors. Similarly, a standalone POINT spatial kernel is not admitted to the
current exact-distillation primitive graph; connected POINT bilinears must be
realized through the supported generalized propagation route. Mixed POINT/LapH
transitions that cannot be represented by these existing resources are rejected
at this boundary rather than assigned a guessed tensor realization.

Each primitive tensor occurrence carries two different identities. The
\emph{node identity} distinguishes the occurrence inside this particular Wick
term, while the \emph{resource identity} describes the reusable mathematical
tensor that the occurrence requires. Two kernel occurrences, for example, may
have the same spatial resource identity but different node identities because
they occupy different places in the term. The primitive-graph stage
intentionally preserves both occurrences. No deduplication is performed merely
because their resource identifiers agree.

This distinction is necessary because resource equality and tensor occurrence
are different statements. A Wick term may use the same perambulator resource
more than once, and those two uses must retain distinct contractions even though
they may ultimately be materialized from the same external data source. The
same applies to repeated spatial kernels and spin matrices. Occurrence-specific
ports and index bindings therefore remain attached to each node until a later
global analysis proves what can be reused without changing the contraction.

The graph is validated before it is passed to optimization. Node identifiers
must be unique, a tensor cannot expose the same local port twice, contraction
indices cannot be empty, and no global index may have degree greater than two.
A graph in which every index has degree two is a closed scalar contraction;
degree-one indices define its external interface. These checks establish the
well-formed numerical tensor network independently of any proposed contraction
path.

SymCorr constructs one such \texttt{WickContractionGraph} for every complete
PhysicsIR Wick term. Equal primitive resources therefore remain visible across
terms and across correlation-matrix elements, but they have not yet been
combined. The next stage operates on this collection globally: it deduplicates
exactly equal resource requirements, canonicalizes reusable connected tensor
components, and aggregates exactly equal complete terms before contraction-order
selection. Those operations belong to the execution and optimization framework
in Sec.~\ref{sec:execution}; they are deliberately separated from the task
construction described here.

At the end of this subsection the physics lowering is complete. The compiler has
fixed the coefficient, the required primitive tensor resources, and their exact
index connectivity. It has not chosen pairwise contraction order, common
intermediate expressions, execution schedule, memory buffers, hardware device,
or numerical tensor values. Those remaining decisions can therefore optimize an
already fixed tensor problem without modifying the physical correlator.

\subsection{Backend-independent execution representation}

The execution layer begins from the collection of primitive Wick-contraction
graphs constructed in Sec.~\ref{sec:distillation_lowering}. Its first purpose is
to turn those term-local tensor networks into one hardware-independent
representation of the complete requested calculation. The representation is
progressively refined through global exact reuse, contraction-order selection,
partial-intermediate reuse, and finally an explicit logical dependency graph.
At every one of these stages the compiler is still describing mathematical
values and operations rather than a schedule for a particular CPU or GPU.

The first global representation is a \texttt{GlobalComputationDAG}. Primitive
resource requirements are identified by exact resource identity, while tensor
occurrences retain their operator-slot placement. Connected pieces of the
primitive tensor network are canonicalized independently of temporary node names
and dummy contraction-index labels. One canonical component computation can
therefore be referenced by several Wick terms or several correlation-matrix
elements without treating occurrence-specific placement as part of the reusable
resource itself. The requested correlators remain explicit as sums of Wick
terms, so global reuse does not collapse distinct physical outputs.

Each unique connected component is then assigned a backend-independent
contraction plan. SymCorr presently delegates the algebraic path search to
\texttt{opt\_einsum}, but stores the result in its own compiler representation.
A plan is an ordered sequence of contraction or reduction steps; every step
records its input values and interfaces, the indices eliminated at that step,
the output interface, an estimated operation count, and the size of the
resulting intermediate. This separates the mathematical contraction tree from
the library used to discover it and leaves room for a different path optimizer
without changing the surrounding compiler stages.

Contraction-path search requires numerical dimensions in order to estimate
cost. In the current exact-distillation model, LapH-mode indices have dimension
\(N_v\) for the job and spin indices have dimension four. These dimensions are
provided through a size model used for path selection,

\begin{equation}
|i|=
\begin{cases}
N_v, & i\in\text{LapH-mode space},\\
4,   & i\in\text{Dirac-spin space}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:execution_index_size_model}
\end{equation}

They are optimization inputs rather than part of tensor-resource identity. A
change in \(N_v\), or in the path-search strategy, can therefore change the
chosen contraction order without changing the symbolic correlator or its
primitive tensor connectivity.

Once a contraction tree has been selected, SymCorr rewrites its intermediate
values into a second global DAG. Primitive tensor values are distinguished by
both reusable resource identity and exact logical placement, while contraction
subtrees are canonicalized from their recursively defined inputs and interfaces.
Temporary component step names and dummy local indices are excluded from this
identity. As a result, two algebraically identical partial contractions can
refer to the same logical intermediate even when they arose in different
component plans. Importantly, this pass does not alter the contraction order
that was already selected; it only recognizes identical values inside those
frozen trees.

The final compiler representation before scheduling is the
\texttt{LogicalExecutionDAG}. It makes every remaining operation explicit. Its
nodes fall into four classes:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
v_r & = \operatorname{EvalPrimitive}(r;\pi),\\
v_m & = \operatorname{Contract}(v_{a_1},\ldots,v_{a_k}),\\
w_\alpha & = c_\alpha\prod_j v_{\alpha j},\\
C_i & = \sum_{\alpha\in i} w_\alpha,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:logical_execution_node_classes}
\end{equation}

where \(r\) is a primitive resource, \(\pi\) is its logical operator placement,
\(v_m\) is one reusable contraction intermediate, \(w_\alpha\) is one Wick-term
product, and \(C_i\) is one requested correlator output. Repeated uses of the
same value remain distinct occurrences in an operation when multiplicity is
part of the algebra, for example when the same disconnected scalar component
appears twice in one Wick term.

The logical DAG therefore answers a precise question: \emph{what values must be
produced, and which earlier values does each operation depend on?} Primitive
nodes have no dependencies; contraction nodes depend on their input values;
Wick-term nodes multiply their component results by the already fixed
coefficient; correlator-sum nodes depend on the contributing Wick terms. The
output map identifies the final node corresponding to every requested
correlator. Dependency cycles, missing values, incompatible value interfaces,
and inconsistent Wick-term or correlator output indices are rejected by the
logical-DAG validator.

A backend-neutral static analysis can subsequently derive exact logical tensor
metadata from this DAG. Mode and spin index spaces remain explicit even when
they happen to have the same numerical dimension, and each value receives its
logical shape, element count, scalar type, byte estimate, producer/consumer
relationships, fanout, and distance to an output. This analysis still describes
one logical value at one exact placement; time batching, data residency, file
layout, and physical memory slots are deliberately absent.

No representation described in this subsection is a physical execution plan.
In particular, a topological ordering of the logical DAG merely demonstrates
that its dependencies are acyclic; it is not the selected runtime schedule.
Likewise, estimated FLOPs and intermediate sizes used during contraction-path
search do not choose a device or allocate memory. Scheduling, lifetime-aware
buffer reuse, time-context batching, staging, and hardware feasibility are
introduced only by the later planning stages described below. This boundary
allows those deployment choices to change while preserving the same optimized
mathematical execution DAG.

\subsection{Reuse of repeated structures}
\label{sec:reuse_repeated_structures}

A central capability of the \textsc{SymCorr} framework is the identification and elimination of redundant computational work across an entire correlation matrix. Rather than treating each matrix element \(C_{ij}(t-t_0)\) and each contributing Wick term as an independent contraction program, \textsc{SymCorr} formulates the global calculation as a unified computation directed acyclic graph (\texttt{GlobalComputationDAG}). This subsection explains how Wick-contracted terms are represented as attributed graphs, the multi-tier identifier hierarchy used throughout the compiler, the graph-isomorphism algorithm that discovers topologically equivalent contractions, and how this optimization operates in practical hadron correlation matrices.

\subsubsection{Graph representation of hadron contractions: The proton example}
\label{sec:graph_rep_proton}

To illustrate the graph formulation concretely, consider the rest-frame, positive-parity nucleon (proton) interpolating operator,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal O_N(t) = \epsilon_{abc} \left[ u^a(t)^\top C\gamma_5 d^b(t) \right] P_+ u^c(t),
\label{eq:proton_interpolator_def}
\end{equation}
where \(C = \gamma_2\gamma_4\) is the charge-conjugation matrix, \(\epsilon_{abc}\) is the totally antisymmetric \(SU(3)\) color tensor, and \(P_+ = \frac{1}{2}(\mathbb I + \gamma_4)\) is the positive-parity energy projector. The corresponding Euclidean two-point correlation function is
\begin{equation}
C_{NN}(t, t_0) = \left\langle \mathcal O_N(t) \, \overline{\mathcal O}_N(t_0) \right\rangle,
\label{eq:proton_correlator_def}
\end{equation}
where \(\overline{\mathcal O}_N(t_0) = \mathcal O_N(t_0)^\dagger \gamma_4\) denotes the adjoint source operator.

Because the proton contains two valence up quarks of identical flavor, Wick's theorem generates two distinct contraction terms (\(N_u! \times N_d! = 2! \times 1! = 2\)):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Direct term}: with fermionic permutation sign \(+1\), where up quark~1 at the source propagates into up quark~1 inside the sink diquark, while up quark~2 propagates as the spectator quark.
    \item \textbf{Exchange term}: with fermionic permutation sign \(-1\), where the two up-quark lines cross: up quark~1 at the source propagates into the sink spectator, while up quark~2 propagates into the sink diquark.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Identifier taxonomy in the compiler}
\label{sec:identifier_taxonomy}

To manage data reuse, compilation, and execution without name clashes or ambiguous sharing, \textsc{SymCorr} maintains four strictly decoupled tiers of identifiers:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{correlator\_id}}: Identifies the high-level physical correlator requested by the user in the job declaration (e.g., \texttt{c\_proton} or \texttt{C\_0\_1}).
    \item \textbf{\texttt{node\_id}}: A diagram-local occurrence identifier assigned to a specific tensor node within one un-optimized Wick graph (e.g., \texttt{kernel\_0}, \texttt{propagation\_1}, \texttt{spin\_3}).
    \item \textbf{\texttt{resource\_id}}: A globally unique identifier for the underlying physical/numerical data source. It is generated from a deterministic SHA-256 hash of the mathematical specification (e.g., \texttt{spatial\_kernel\_}\allowbreak\texttt{6427fc17\dots} and \texttt{spatial\_kernel\_}\allowbreak\texttt{63b068c2\dots} for the two proton baryon blocks, one built from $V$ at the sink and one from $V^\dagger$ at the source, and \texttt{perambulator\_}\allowbreak\texttt{cfa5757f\dots} and \texttt{perambulator\_}\allowbreak\texttt{b3fea9be\dots} for the up- and down-quark perambulators; the values are those of the present version). The direct and exchange graphs have distinct node occurrences but share exactly these \texttt{resource\_id} values.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{computation\_id}} (or \textbf{\texttt{component\_id}}): Identifies a unique, canonicalized, connected tensor contraction network (e.g., \texttt{component\_}\allowbreak\texttt{9b66414a\dots} for the direct graph and \texttt{component\_}\allowbreak\texttt{301a5cf4\dots} for the exchange graph). This identifier is invariant under dummy index renaming and node reordering.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Graph isomorphism: Identifying identical contractions through canonical labeling}
\label{sec:isomorphism_strategy}

Two contraction graphs may represent the exact same mathematical contraction, yet appear entirely distinct to a computer. This occurs for two simple reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dummy index naming}: Consider two mathematically identical contractions,
    \begin{equation*}
    \sum_\alpha \tau_{\alpha\beta} \, (C\gamma_5)_{\beta\alpha} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \sum_\mu (C\gamma_5)_{\nu\mu} \, \tau_{\mu\nu}.
    \end{equation*}
    Although they compute the same tensor value, an automated parser sees different dummy labels (\(\alpha,\beta\) versus \(\mu,\nu\)).
    \item \textbf{Arbitrary node order}: Tensors in memory can appear in any sequence (listing \(\tau\) before \(C\gamma_5\) or vice versa) without altering the physical contraction result.
\end{enumerate}

To recognize identical contractions across different correlators, \textsc{SymCorr} converts every contraction graph into a unique, standardized form---a process known as \emph{canonical labeling}.

Redstar~\cite{10.1145/3592979.3593409} performs this step with the general-purpose canonical-labeling package \texttt{nauty}. Lattice QCD contraction graphs are, however, compact, typically containing only $5$--$25$ tensor nodes, so that an exhaustive search over starting nodes is affordable. \textsc{SymCorr} therefore uses a lightweight, self-contained canonicalization strategy based on three intuitive steps:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Step 1: Graph traversal and index standardization}: Starting from a chosen tensor, the compiler walks through the graph by following port connections in a fixed numerical port order (port~0, port~1, \ldots). As internal connections and open indices are encountered along the walk, they are renamed systematically to sequential canonical labels (\(i_0, i_1, i_2, \ldots\) for contracted internal indices and \(o_0, o_1, \ldots\) for open external indices).
    \item \textbf{Step 2: Unbiased root selection}: Because a loop of contracted tensors has no natural ``first'' node, \textsc{SymCorr} tries starting the walk from every candidate tensor in the graph. Each choice of starting node produces a standardized candidate string describing the walk.
    \item \textbf{Step 3: Deterministic tie-breaking (Lexicographical minimum)}: Among all candidate description strings generated across all possible starting nodes, the compiler deterministically selects the one that is alphabetically earliest (the lexicographical minimum).
\end{itemize}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Canonical Graph Isomorphism in \textsc{SymCorr}}
\label{alg:canonical_graph_isomorphism}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Contraction graph \(G\) with tensor nodes, port definitions, and index connections.
\Ensure Canonical identifier \texttt{computation\_id} and standardized contraction plan.
\State \textbf{Initialize} candidate list \(\mathcal C \gets [\,]\).
\For{each candidate starting tensor \(r\) in \(G\)}
    \State Walk through connected tensors starting from \(r\), following port connections in fixed numerical port order.
    \State Record the sequential visit order of tensors \((t_0, t_1, \ldots)\).
    \State Encode tensor kinds, underlying resource IDs, and relative edge steps into a standardized string \(S_r\).
    \State Add \(S_r\) to candidate list \(\mathcal C\).
\EndFor
\State \textbf{Select} the alphabetically earliest candidate:
\[ S_{\mathrm{min}} \gets \min(\mathcal C) \]
\State \textbf{Standardize indices}: Rename internal contracted indices to \(\{i_0, i_1, i_2, \dots\}\) and open indices to \(\{o_0, o_1, \dots\}\) according to the winning walk.
\State \textbf{Generate fingerprint}: Compute the cryptographic hash:
\[ \texttt{computation\_id} \gets \operatorname{SHA-256}(S_{\mathrm{min}})[:20] \]
\State \Return Standardized contraction component and its \texttt{computation\_id}.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Because this procedure exhaustively tests all starting tensors and deterministically selects the lexicographically minimal description, the resulting string \(S_{\mathrm{min}}\) and its hash \texttt{computation\_id} serve as a canonical fingerprint: two contraction graphs receive the same \texttt{computation\_id} exactly when they are identical up to the order of their tensors and the names of their indices, with the same tensor kinds, resources and port connections. Whenever two hashes agree, the full strings are compared, so that a hash collision cannot merge different graphs. Equalities that rely on tensor symmetries or on physics relations such as Hermiticity are not detected by this structural test.

Once graphs are fingerprinted in this way, the compiler tests for graph equivalence with a single \(O(1)\) hash lookup. If two graphs share the same \texttt{computation\_id}, the tensor contraction is scheduled and computed only once, and the result is reused wherever needed in the global correlation matrix.

\subsubsection{Reuse in a nucleon correlation matrix}
\label{sec:cross_correlator_isomorphism}

Where does graph isomorphism provide practical computational reuse in realistic lattice QCD spectroscopy? Consider a $2 \times 2$ correlation matrix of spin-$1/2^+$ rest-frame nucleon interpolators constructed from two distinct diquark structures: a pseudoscalar diquark operator \(\mathcal O_1(t)\) and an axial-vector/temporal diquark operator \(\mathcal O_2(t)\),
\begin{align}
\mathcal O_1(t) &= \epsilon_{abc} \left[ u^a(t)^\top C\gamma_5 d^b(t) \right] P_+ u^c(t), \label{eq:op1_nucleon}\\
\mathcal O_2(t) &= \epsilon_{abc} \left[ u^a(t)^\top C\gamma_4\gamma_5 d^b(t) \right] P_+ u^c(t). \label{eq:op2_nucleon}
\end{align}
The four matrix elements evaluated across the correlation matrix are
\begin{equation}
\mathbf C(t, t_0) = 
\begin{pmatrix}
C_{11}(t, t_0) & C_{12}(t, t_0) \\
C_{21}(t, t_0) & C_{22}(t, t_0)
\end{pmatrix},
\label{eq:nucleon_2x2_matrix}
\end{equation}
where each correlator element is defined by \(C_{ij}(t, t_0) = \langle \mathcal O_i(t) \, \overline{\mathcal O}_j(t_0) \rangle\).

Each of the four correlators contains two Wick terms, so the matrix produces eight contraction graphs
with 96 tensor occurrences in total. The global compiler pass identifies the following structure:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Shared tensors across the full basis}:
    Because spin matrices are not part of the spatial kernels, all eight graphs use the same two baryon
    kernels, one built from $V$ for the sink block and one from $V^\dagger$ for the source block, and the
    same up- and down-quark perambulators. Together with seven distinct spin matrices, the 96 tensor
    occurrences require only 11 distinct tensors, each of which is loaded or built once per time slice.

    \item \textbf{Distinct contraction networks}:
    In this example all eight networks are distinct. In particular, the off-diagonal elements
    \(C_{12}\) and \(C_{21}\), which are related by Hermiticity on the gauge ensemble, carry different
    spin matrices at the sink and the source (\(C\gamma_5\) at the sink and the conjugate of
    \(C\gamma_4\gamma_5\) at the source, or vice versa). The structural test of
    Algorithm~\ref{alg:canonical_graph_isomorphism} therefore treats them, correctly, as different
    computations. Exploiting relations of this kind would require an additional, separately validated
    rule and is not done.

    \item \textbf{Exact algebraic cancellation (zero pruning)}:
    Within a single correlator sum, if two contributing scalar terms possess identical canonical components but opposite coefficients (\(c_\alpha + c_\beta = 0\)), \textsc{SymCorr} sums the coefficients and prunes the term from the execution DAG, so that no operations or buffer memory are spent on vanishing contributions.
\end{enumerate}

Reuse of identical networks becomes important when the same operator structures recur across many
matrix elements and Wick terms, and partial contractions shared between different networks provide a
further reduction (Sec.~\ref{sec:execution}). The reductions obtained for larger systems are
quantified in Sec.~\ref{sec:performance}.
